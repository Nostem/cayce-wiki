Date: 5/6/1936  Sex: F  ReadingID: 8140

This psychic reading given by Edgar Cayce at his home on Arctic Crescent, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 6th day of May, 1936, in accordance with request made by [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]], Manager of the Ass''n for Research & Enlightenment, Inc., and Thomas B. House, Manager of the Health Home Remedies Corporation.

[Edgar Cayce; [[Gertrude Cayce|Gertrude Cayce]], Conductor; [[Gladys Davis|Gladys Davis]], Steno. [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]] and Thomas B. House.]

Time of Reading                            308-9 Essex Bldg., 
12:50 to 1:20 P. M. Eastern Standard Time. Norfolk, Va. 
(Preparations being manufactured by the H.H.R. Corp. 
under the supervision of T. B. House, also the experiments, 
connections and research work in connection with these 
products.  Questions regarding each product.)

1. EC:  Yes, we have the preparations, those experiments, those associations, connections, and the enquiring mind of T. B. House, present in this room.

2. Ready for questions.

3. (Q)  First:  T-I-M, a remedy for hemorrhoids, a jar of which T. B. House holds in his hand.  Does this product carry enough Tincture of Benzoin to act as an effective astringent in reducing the surplus skin or sac left by the hemorrhoid after it has been assimilated in the system?
(A)  In this particular jar here, not sufficient; though it will act as an astringent.  But a small quantity added to same would make it more effective, though in some types more irritating.

4. (Q)  Should the amount of Benzoin now being used according to the formula that has been given be increased for general distribution?
(A)  Should be increased about one percent.

5. (Q)  How many drops to each ounce should it be increased?
(A)  In the preparation of this there is a great deal of difference depending upon the time it is added to the solution, see?  and in the temperature and in the condition of the oil that is reduced from the butterfat, or the butterfat oil; the condition of this as the base, as well as the quantity of the tobacco that is absorbed by same - or snuff.  And when this is added then, to the whole amount we would add - for the ounce - five more drops.  Instead of from six then to twelve, add fifteen drops.  For in this the proportion is ten.

6. (Q)  Please explain the reactions and effect of the [[Atomidine|Atomidine]] and the Snuff in this preparation.
(A)  The [[Atomidine|Atomidine]] being broken up from kelp (carrying an effective activity upon irritation) makes for an activity upon the tobacco, which also is as a preservative as well as a deodorant, as well as an activity that enhances the action of the atomic forces in the [[Iodine|iodine]], see?

7. (Q)  At what temperature of the Butterfat should the Benzoin be added?
(A)  Hundred and one degrees.

8. (Q)  At what temperature should the [[Atomidine|Atomidine]] be added?
(A)  At ninety-eight.

9. (Q)  Is a sufficient quantity of the Atomidine being used?
(A)  Sufficient quantity, provided this same strength is kept.

10. (Q)  Is the one percent solution of the Atomidine the proper amount?
(A)  There's more than that in this!

11. (Q)  What percent solution of Atomidine should be used in this?
(A)  The REGULAR solution that is put up for commercial use, which makes it about one and one-half percent of the atomic forces in same.

12. (Q)  Is the pack of Tobacco now in the office of the Health Home Remedies Corp. as good to use as Snuff?
(A)  No.

13. (Q)  Do you recommend any changes in this product for use in internal hemorrhoids?
(A)  Necessary that those put in tubes be MORE of the Butterfat and less of the Tobacco.

14. (Q)  Just how much should the Tobacco be cut down?
(A)  When this is put in the solution, it would be stirred until it is the consistency of soft soap - or in proportion this would be, for each ounce of Butterfat there would be added half and a quarter of the Tobacco, see, or Snuff.

15. (Q)  Which of the two types of tubes held by T. B. House is best suited from its composition to use as package for Tim?
(A)  That which has the most lead and the less of tin, see?

This is a little bit more expensive but the activity or the action of properties as they are packed will be held more into their normal state, see?

16. (Q)  Is the tube now held by T. B. House the best to be used of any type for this product?
(A)  Well, there's many different TYPES - but this will be correct.

17. (Q)  Is my present method of approach in contacting doctors the proper one to use?  Advise.
(A)  This will naturally mean that personalities are to be dealt with, in contacting those that may become users or suggestive users.  These are not the greater numbers to whom or even through whom the product will be distributed, but these are necessary for their reports, for their aid, for their help in the various ways or manners that will ENABLE the body, or those distributing same, to know the BETTER approach to individual users.  Or in the preparing of data, or preparing of ads that WOULD be the means of influencing those who are sufferers from such, to at least give the try.

As to the approach to these, these will be individual, meeting the personalities as they are found.

In the general it's very good.  The changes - use common sense in personal approach.

18. (Q)  Any other suggestions for improving Tim?
(A)  These may come after preparations have been attempted or tried, and various characters of containers and the effect of oxidization of same in same.  But in the main this is ready for distribution.

19. (Q)  Second:  Ipsab, a remedy for pyorrhea.  A report [1800-26, Par. R1] submitted by the Norfolk Testing Laboratories is held by T. B. House, which indicates the results obtained from bacteriological tests on streptococcus and bacilli germs with Ipsab.  Should Ipsab kill these germs, if so what is wrong with the product as prepared by us?
(A)  These are preparations that are made for the activity upon another form, as has been indicated.  If it is the desire that these are to be included, then these are to be changed somewhat in the formula of the preparation. Increasing the strength of the solution of the toothache bark, as well as increasing the quantity of Atomidine and its strength IN the solution, see?  and these will destroy those also!

20. (Q)  Is it advisable to do this, considering our present formula and its value for distribution?
(A)  It may be well to do so, if this is desired to be activative in this kind or character.  Or it may be used the TINCTURE of [[Iodine|Iodine]] and thus require that there be a different CHARACTER of test made when these are combined in their order.  But STRENGTHENING the solution or the elixir of the bark, strengthening the quantity of the Atomidine will make it for a more activative influence upon such.  But not more effective upon the germ of the pyorrhea.

And these then become at times in specific cases or other character of cases an irritant.  But these - should they be required - we would make a density of seven (this is double the strength, almost doubling the strength); but it will require a different form or manner of extracting the juices from the properties, see?  And to the quantity of the Atomidine that would be the ordinary strength, or half the quantity of the CONCENTRATED solution, see?  using the concentrated solution as may be procured for such, using the same quantity or adding the ten minims to the ounce MORE, see?

21. (Q)  Do we understand ten minims more than is being used now, of the concentrated solution?
(A)  Of the concentrated solution ten minims more than is being used now of the REGULAR strength.

Another condition here - as this is a portion of that:

Do not change the specific gravity or the quantity of the calcium, see; but INCREASE the density of the seawater, by REDUCING (by boiling, see) before the other properties are added.  Why?

The seawater carries many properties that CHANGE, see, as to the varied effects, the character, where it is obtained, how it may be obtained, etc.  Portions of these are to be destroyed, the bacilli that's in same.  The density of the salt in same is to be intensified, see?  This then will make for a more concentrated solution and more effective upon the activity of bacilli.

But these WILL destroy that which produces Riggs' Diseases.

Reducing the quantity for the gallon to a half, and then adding the other solutions.

22. (Q)  How should the juice of the bark be extracted as indicated?
(A)  Put to soak for twelve hours before it is taken off, or put into the solution, or soak twelve hours and THEN boil, see?  Boiled in the same water it is soaked in, to be sure, and with the other ingredients added until the solution is extracted.

23. (Q)  Please indicate how tests should be made to prove the value of Ipsab.
(A)  Only upon cases where there are disturbances of loosening of the teeth, the bleeding gums; such conditions on individual cases may the tests be made.

24. (Q)  Would the suggestions made by Hunt of the Norfolk Testing Laboratories for testing the antiseptic power of each ingredient be advisable or useful to us?
(A)  Not as INDIVIDUAL tests but as those combined.  For many antiseptic solutions are strengthened in their activity by combinations when they are less effective in their own individual activity.  Others become less effective by combinations, see?

25. (Q)  Should WE attempt to give Ipsab antiseptic value? If so, how should this be done?
(A)  There may be added alcohols for such a [[Purpose|purpose]]. But that these are all natural or nature's preservatives indicates the greater value of this FOR a specific [[Purpose|purpose]]. It is not for the breath, it is not for decayed teeth, it is not for any of the numbers of mouth disturbances, but for disturbed GUMS that ARE affected by certain character of diseases!

If it is desired or required that this be added, then add it to those proportions that it BECOMES then only a ten to twenty percent solution.

26. (Q)  What is the reaction of the Prickly Ash Bark on the gums?
(A)  It is nature's preservative for STRENGTHENING the tissue about the teeth themselves.  The very NATURE of it, from its name as given by the aborigines - that toothache bark indicates its very nature!

27. (Q)  Any suggestions for getting this preparation ready for general distribution?
(A)  Many tests, many varied ways and combinations will be well.  Make them to suit not only laboratory tests (NOT for gargles nor for others) but for the gums; not for whitening teeth.  Rather does it tend to make for a skim, but of such a nature that this as even prepared into a toothpaste, using only [[Iodine|iodine]] AND salt, and then a combination of the properties for a toothpaste in its combination, will make all the others look silly!

28. (Q)  Third:  Hair Curler [See 289-1, Par. 11-A; See 255-13]. T. B. House holds a solution prepared according to formula given through this channel for a hair curler.
(A)  (Interrupting)  Not by a whole jug full!  This is made AT it, but not like the formula given!  First the bark of the same trees from which the fruit are taken, and the fruit or the seed of the fruit are to be crushed.  They are to be crushed when the FRUIT has been taken from same and not when they have been dried or polished or rubbed off, and most of that which goes into the making for the curling solution has been lost!

Do this way and you'll have it correct!

29. We are through for the present.

> Being the Preface to a Volume, Now in Preparation, on the

> "There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the

> It is not here my purpose to strike on the bandor of time
> more notes than were awarded, in the beginning of my plan,
> these minutia of preface.  I had intended to announce in
> brief the purpose of my efforts, the effect toward which
> sentence and paragraph and chapter were aimed.  But it seems,
> as with all things in which man and his mind properly have
> no business, that I am entangled.  Such simplicities as were
> clear to me in their birth have clouded on the pages where
> they were to mature.  Their dress in words cannot be a
> [[Greece|Grecian]] gown, simple and caught at the middle with a golden
> clasp.  They demand designs with which I am not now, nor ever
> will be, familiar.  Yet in the face of challenge I can but
> cross their desires with language, and hope they will come
> forth fulfilled.
> 
> The story of Edgar Cayce is not the chronicle of a man born,
> appointed to a [[Destiny|destiny]], and fulfilling it.  It is not a
> biography built upon facts, garnished with anecdote, and
> served with similitude and interpretation.  It has more to
> itself than that.  It demands, in its telling, the scope and
> poetry of legend, the depth and preciseness of history, the
> clarity of science, the exactness of mathematics, and the
> [[Visions|vision]] of religion.  Such abilities do not come in the same
> human package.  The mind with which I had hoped to mate the
> matter of this tale has no solution for its meanings, its
> ramifications, its subtleties and myriad implications.
> Only by the inflation of a vast insolence, and loud singing
> to drown the voice of fear, can this venture hope to pass
> through the dark valley of verb and come out walking.
> 
> There is contained in the life of every man, whatever his
> caste in the system of human intelligence, a mystery as
> great as the universe:  a formula so intricately woven,
> so cunningly patterned, that even his [[Soul|soul]], groping through
> ages of thought, can but feebly guess at the solution.  The
> architecture of his skeleton, the chemistry of his flesh,
> are phenomena which he has never more than vaguely under-
> stood.  Of his mind he has no knowledge at all, except that
> it operates - objectively upon the life which besets him from
> without subjectively upon the death which gathers within.  He
> has no certain knowledge of how to deal with either of these
> exigencies, and in puzzlement he blends them, to his further
> bewilderment.  He labors in the life without him; in order to
> preserve the life within him; yet the execution of this labor
> is the matter upon which the death that grows within him
> feeds.  His existence is a paradox:  he moves inexorably
> toward extinction, laboring that he may preserve himself
> for it.  He works that he may live that he may die.
> 
> To the beast and the flower this is not an irrational
> procedure.  There are other beasts and other flowers,
> descendants of those which return to earth.  Nor does it
> seem unreasonable to the average man that people in [[China|China]]

> itself to the individual that mind fails to follow the
> pathways of matter.  It is something with which reason,
> however fortified by evidence, will have nothing to do.

> argument; of this it is afraid.  The first man with a [[Soul|soul]],

> and the rivers and the forests:  a life wherein there was
> always harvest, and where age could not enter in.  He told
> it, before he died, to his sons and daughters and wife, and
> said he would meet them there.  They believed him, and they
> were happy.
> 
> We, too, have believed that story, and we have been happy
> because of it.  We have examined the architecture of our
> skeletons and the chemistry of our flesh, and have found no
> answer to them.  We have divided our minds into squares and
> circles and put names upon them, but nothing has come of it.
> We have questioned each other closely about the thoughts that
> have been engendered within us, and have found no cause for
> joy.  In the end we have accepted the word of the witnesses
> of death, who have told us that peace comes to the faces of
> those who are about to travel into nothingness.  With this
> as our guide we have carried our ancestors and our lovers and
> our friends to their graves, and have prayed for them.  We
> have built churches, and taken to ourselves religion.  We
> have made our hope into a belief, and our belief into a
> social structure.  We have taught others to believe as we
> believe, for in numbers there is strength.  We do not wish
> to go into this nothingness alone.
> 
> Yet when the city lights have receded and the doors of
> our churches are closed we stand alone with doubt.  We can
> gaze through telescopes at stars greater than our sun, and
> speculate on other worlds in the space toward which some
> day we will be propelled.  We can see upon the blackboard
> of a schoolroom scrawling numbers which prove that space
> and matter and life and death are one.  We can pass swiftly
> through the air, or over our lands, or through the seas,
> or even underground.  We can receive from the hands of the
> chemist substances which restore our health.  We can hear in
> our homes the voices of men a thousand miles away.  But from
> none of these can we receive surcease from our end, from none
> of these can we gain knowledge to illumine our doubt.  With
> this we must grapple alone.
> 
> The facts are few.  At some unspecified time we emerge into
> a world with which we are not familiar, and with which we are
> not equipped to cope.  At another unspecified time we leave
> it.  We are, when we die, somewhat larger in body than when
> we are born, but this has no bearing on our contemplation
> of the two events.  Before birth we had nothing to do with
> our bodies, and after death we have nothing to do with them.
> What, then, are we?  We are apparently our thoughts.  When we
> do not have them we do not exist.  When we have them we are
> alive.  We do not remember having had any before our birth,
> nor for some time thereafter.  As we die they flee from us.
> Yet they are we.  How then can they flee from us?  We must,
> perforce, go with them.  But where?
> 
> They may be, these thoughts, only another of our senses.
> These, we know die.  They pass away slowly, painlessly,
> leaving us without feeling, without sight, without hearing,
> without taste, without smell.  When we are young they are
> strong and alert.  They pick up the world for us, and bring
> it to our minds.  When their work is done they begin to
> sleep, and then to die.  Our eyes grow dim, our touch
> is muddy, our ears and our taste buds and our noses are
> untrustworthy.  We can no longer smell spring in the air,
> nor feel it against our feet, nor sight it through the bark
> of trees.  We grow old, and only remembrance of things makes
> them real.
> 
> As my fingers strike the keys of the typewriter which is
> translating these thought impulses into words, tiny cells are
> brushed off my finger ends.  These cells, which were a part
> of me, are destroyed.  So soon as they leave me, however,
> trophic nerves, which carry impulses throughout the body,
> send an SOS to headquarters.  Immediately the blood supply
> is marshalled for service, cells are put into swift-flowing
> streams, and dropped into the empty spaces.  The tissue is
> repaired, and I have no knowledge that damage was done.
> 
> If I suffered from anesthetic leprosy this repair would not
> take place.  My trophic nerves would not send any message,
> and my body would be unaware that help was needed.  No cells
> would replace those worn off by the keys, and when I had
> completed the life of Edgar Cayce my fingers would be stubs.
> 
> That is what happens to our senses.  As life proceeds
> from the simplicities of childhood to the complexities of
> adolescence, maturity, and senility, we become confused.  An
> odor that was once the perfume of the roses becomes a snarl
> of memories, a heap of circumstances and happenings in which
> the perfume of roses was in some way concerned.  Our senses,
> besides being duller, are no longer straightforward in their
> accounting to us.  Our eyes no longer see a house in which we
> live.  They see a kaleidoscope of happenings and events and
> people that have had to do with us in that house.  Our taste
> buds no longer tell us that we are drinking Rhine wine.
> They tell us instead that we are sitting on a sidewalk in
> Budapest, watching the Danube and the people who are passing
> by.  The senses have given over to their own death.  They
> are immersed in a pile of memories, recognizing things only
> because of the remembrances these things incur.
> 
> Likewise the mind, struggling to extricate itself from this
> mass of wreckage, becomes confused.  Desperate to escape,
> it denies itself:  it says it has no part in the wreckage,
> it calls what is past the [[Subconscious Mind|subconscious]].  It divides and
> re-divides itself, making excuses for its incompetencies,
> tracing its mistakes to prenatal influence and heredity,
> blaming its dullness on environment, seeking to escape
> through a process of mental suasion that depends for its
> rationality upon irrationality.  In the end it comes face
> to face with itself again, having rounded the block in its
> flight and arrived, panting and exhausted, precisely at the
> point where it began.  It is inevitably itself, existing
> without effort, but unsure of its future.  The doubt remains.
> 
> All of these things are known to each of us, and each of us,
> now and again, ponders them.  In our youth we talk of them to
> other persons, until we perceive that these other persons are
> precisely as muddled as we are, and as fearful.  Then we take
> the task within us, and thereafter turn it over and exercise
> it, frequently at first, then with less and less regularity,
> and finally at only those times when the hand of the potter
> seems about the crush us in the mold.
> 
> It was my fortune when, in my now quite faraway youth I was
> putting these things to myself and others, that a listener
> happened in who was not of the common herd.  He seemed,
> though as young as I, to be unworried.  He smiled tolerantly
> at my voiced heresies - wild cries of fear that I tried to
> make into shouts of courage.  To my inchoate philosophies he
> made quiet answer, puncturing their tender shells with barbs
> of what I forthwith considered genius.  I took him to my
> bosom.  Through long nights of the 1926 winter in Lexington,
> Virginia, in our tiny rooms in Lees Dormitory, when we
> should have been preparing lessons for the stern masters
> of Washington and Lee University, we talked of the doubt.
> 
> He said he knew, this bespecaled young man who so willingly
> gave up sleep to instruct me.  I asked him how he knew.  Then
> for days he would not talk to me, and I saw him brooding
> over his books, staring at me occasionally with opaque eyes,
> staring at me occasionally with eyes that shot into me and
> stabbed at my nerves.  Then one night he told me how he knew.
> 
> "My father," he said, "is a psychic medium."
> 
> It is now a long time since that happened, and I do not
> remember much except that we stayed up all night, and that
> at breakfast we were still talking.  I wanted to know, but
> I did not believe.  I was a [[Rome|Roman]] Catholic Irishman from
> Connecticut.  He, Hugh Lynn Cayce, was a Christian Church
> member from Virginia.  There was no valid reason why we
> should like each other, why we should be together.  It
> was, I thought afterward, almost indecent.
> 
> But he said he knew, and he talked of a God Who was merciful,
> Who was understanding, Who saw eye to eye with my blundering
> desires in life.  So we forsook sleep on other nights, and
> talked again, and again.
> 
> He told me of a man, his father, who lay down to sleep and
> spoke with the voice of authority:  who cured the halt and
> the lame and the sick and the blind; who told the story
> of the world before history began; who traced in the minds
> of those who came to ask him, other lives on earth and the
> promise of more lives to be; who spoke of the [[Old Covenant|Old Testament]]
> and the [[New Covenant|New Testament]] as truth; who made heaven and salvation
> a reward for the strong, not a dull fortress in an
> inconceivable land manned by the wimpering and the cowardly
> who had prayed and given alms and fled from battle.
> 
> So in that spring I went with the boy to Virginia Beach,
> Virginia, to see his father.  I expected a dark-haired,
> sharp-eyed, stalwart man who walked with gods and spoke in
> the language of metaphysics.  I met a mild, amiable, soft
> spoken man who on the first day changed his cigarette brand
> to suit mine, who allowed me to teach him the intricacies of
> rummy, and who asked me to go fishing with him.  He took me
> down town and bought me a [[Baking Soda|soda]], introduced me to the village
> barber, and played three sets of tennis with me.  I went to
> bed in his house that night with complete peace.  Such a
> nice man, I knew, would not let [[Fallen Angels|the devil]] get me.
> 
> All that, too, seems a long time ago.  He has been my friend
> now for nearly ten years.  I am so conversant with his life
> and with his work that I begin where I began myself, and
> explain it step by step, seems a confusing and arduous task.
> Perhaps I should have done it long ago, before I was so lost
> in the trees that I could not see the forest.  Perhaps I
> should not ever have attempted it.
> 
> Yet there is, in my files, an envelope labeled, "Readings;"
> and there is, in my mind and in my heart, a certain peace.
> 
> These things are due to Edgar Cayce.  Since that day when
> we played rummy and discussed cigarettes and drank [[Baking Soda|soda]] and
> played tennis, no one of the questions which arise in the
> dark hours to plague the minds and hearts of men has troubled
> me.  The soundness of my body, the temper of my mind, the
> quiet of my [[Soul|soul]], are due to him.  He has slept for me many
> times, with the sun across his face, and he has told me who
> I am and what I ought to be, and who I was in the years that
> are history and legend.
> 
> He has informed me of the reasons for my ills and my
> frettings; and given me ointments for their heal.  He has
> calmed me when I was unruly, and praised me when I was
> meek.  He has set my feet again and again on the highway
> of humility, and held over and over again that mirror before
> my face in which is reflected the charge of my existence.
> These things he did when asleep.  Waking, he has given me
> roof and sustenence, friendship and company.  We have walked
> together through the canyons of Manhattan and over the sands
> of Virginia Beach.  We have whiled away the hours on golf
> courses, and on the shaded porches of his home.  He has
> listened to my troubles, and unburdened himself to me.
> Thereis not any man for whom I hold greater love.  He is,
> in brief, my friend.  I believe in him; I believe in his
> labors; I believe in his gifts.
> 
> Therefore this task has come to me.  I had hoped that someone
> more worthy would be appointed.  I know that I am inadequate,
> that never in my life will a more difficult problem be set
> before me.  It deserves better hands.  Yet the time has come
> for its fulfillment, and no other workman is at hand.  I have
> taken it gladly; it is my honor.
> 
> To those who know Edgar Cayce and his work I have this to
> say:  the breadth of the assignment has not escaped me.  I
> know that on any one of the innumerable subjects which have
> coursed through his 25,000 readings a book could be written.
> These books, in time, will be written.  My [[Book of Job|job]] is to break
> the ground, to lay down with as much completeness and
> exactitude as is possible the fundamentals of Edgar Cayce,
> his life, and his work.  Much must be excluded.  Things
> that were momentus in their days of happening seem, in
> perspective, shadowy details that stray into dull byways.
> The outline, massive in years and importance, remains.  It
> is this which I hope to get solidly planted between covers.
> The rest, in due course, will follow.
> 
> To those who come upon Edgar Cayce for the first time let
> this be warning:  it is no tale to take in stride.  Whatever
> else it gives you, it will give you pause.  There is not
> in the chapters which will follow any program of ballyhoo,
> any attempt to sell a product.  This is a document for the
> purpose of record.  Those who would quibble, who would point
> the finger of their intelligence in scorn, who would call
> questions and cry for proof, had best leave off and take
> again to their school books.  Here is a river for those who
> wish to bathe; but those who enter it had best know how to
> swim.
> 
> Thomas Sugrue
> June 15, 1936
> New York City.
