# TEXT OF READING 254-91

**Reading [[254-91]]** · Series: [[250-261 — Mental and Spiritual Development (Individual)|250-261]] · Year: 1936 · Sex: M

## Text

Date: 6/28/1936  Sex: M  ReadingID: 1229

This psychic reading given by Edgar Cayce at his home on Arctic Crescent, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 28th day of June, 1936, in accordance with request by those present at this meeting of the Fifth Annual Congress of the Ass''n for Research & Enlightenment, Inc.

[Edgar Cayce; [[Gertrude Cayce|Gertrude Cayce]], Conductor; [[Gladys Davis|Gladys Davis]], Steno. [[Gladys Davis|Gladys]] & Douglas Johnston, [[King David|David]] E. [[David Kahn|Kahn]], Helen & Alec Ellington, Jane (Minnie) Williams, Myrtle & R. L. Demaio, Hannah Miller, [[Virgin Mary|Mary]] Blood, Chester Robinson, Florence & Edith Edmonds, Esther Wynne, Helen Storey, Ethel & Edgar Parry, Anne & [[John (Apostle)|John]] Penn, Lillian Barclay, Frances Y. Morrow, Ruth LeNoir, Jennie Moore, Henry Hardwicke, [[Zacharias and Elizabeth|Elizabeth]] Perry, Abbie Kemp, & [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]]; also Sally Jones.]

Time of Reading 
4:00 to 4:15 P. M. Eastern Standard Time.

1. GC:  You will have before you the Ass'n for Research & Enlightenment, Inc., members of which are present here.  You will consider the work of this organization in studying and presenting the psychic work of Edgar Cayce.  You will give at this time a discourse which will set forth the standards for our activities during the coming year and giving such specific suggestions and council as may be helpful to the members of this Association.

2. EC:  Yes, we have the [[Purpose|purposes]] of [[A.R.E. (Association for Research and Enlightenment)|the Association]] for Research and Enlightenment, the members that are present; as a group or a membership and as individuals.

3. In considering the work of such an organization and its standards, that as composed of individuals of all character of experience, all influences that are of a nature that has brought about the interest in a work of that purposed by the activity of such an Association, we find it is - as has been given oft - first to the individual, then to the groups, then to classes and masses.

4. As to the activities that are before this organization, first - as we find - there is a period just ahead in which there will be given the greater opportunities for service by each and every member.  This is not only owing to the general conditions of an unsettled nature but to the termination of a period and the beginning of a period when there is to be the LIVING by individuals of that which has prompted them.  Not as ones seeking for self-exaltation, not as individuals seeking for an easy way, not as individuals seeking for a manner or a means of escaping their own selves; but rather as in using their understanding, their comprehension, their knowledge, their love their patience, their long-suffering, in such ways and manners that there may not indeed be the perishing of hope and faith in the earth.

5. Such opportunities are before all those who have purposed in their heart to DO good unto those not only of the household of faith in their individual tenets or beliefs but unto ALL, because each [[Soul|soul]] that manifests itself in human form IS thy brother - and the spirit and [[Soul|soul]] of same is in the form of thy Maker.  For the Lord's sake, then, the opportunities are not to be used to self's own glory but that the glory of the Father may be made manifest in the earth.

6. THESE be the opportunities, THESE be the experiences through which each and every [[Soul|soul]] will pass within the next year.

7. For there is set before thee those choices to be made, as to WHOM ye will serve.

8. As to MANNERS, then, in which each soul shall conduct self as respecting this as an individual organization, it has oft been given that this is not under any schism or ism or any individual tenet other than that which has been of old, "I AM my brother's keeper!"  That should be the cry that should be in the heart of every member, every individual, "I AM MY BROTHER'S KEEPER!"

9. Then so live, so act, so conduct thyself in thy dealings with thy fellow man day by day that they who are astray may be set aright.  Not those who are astray only according to THINE own interpretations, but those who are seeking!  Art thou astray? Art thou troubled?  Art thou discouraged?  Art thou fainthearted?  If so, then THOU art astray!

10. For "Lo, I am with thee always" is the promise to those who have put on the whole armor.  "If ye will be my children, I will be thy God" is to every soul.  Have ye lost touch?  Have ye lost faith?  Have ye lost hope?  If so, then ye YOURSELVES are astray!

11. In thine dealings with [[A.R.E. (Association for Research and Enlightenment)|the Association]], then, and its [[Purpose|purposes]], and those sources or channels through which ye may seek for that comfort, have ye APPLIED in thy DAILY experience that thou hast heard, that thou hast found good, that thou hast experienced in thine OWN life?  If so, THEN ye may INDEED know the manner, the way in which ye may use thyself in whatever sphere or phase of activity ye find yourselves; that ye may be a BETTER member, a better servant of a living God!  For ye who would be the greatest will indeed be the servant of all, as He hath given from the beginning.  Yea, thy Lord, thy God, showeth thee this day by day in His dealings with thy fellow man, to show those promises that have been made sure in the experiences of those that seek.  For He giveth good and evil unto all, and ye choose through [[Free Will|the WILL]] thy relationships - as to whether they shall be for self-exaltation, self-glorification, self-indulgence, self-gratification, or for the love of life, of truth, of hope, of honor, of virtue, of patience, of brotherly love.  Ye yourselves show forth these in the manners, the ways ye deal with thy fellow man!

12. Forgive, if ye would be forgiven.  Be patient, if ye would have thy Father be patient with thee.

13. In what manner, then?

14. First having convinced thyself through thine own personal application, then, as has been given, that which is good, that which is life, if ye would have same give it unto others.  For in so doing ye gain that understanding that bringeth to thine own experience that which makes for the fulfilling of that [[Purpose|purpose]] for which ye came into this experience.

15. For He hath prepared a way of escape for each soul; He hath not willed that any soul should perish.  Nothing may separate thee from the knowledge save thyself!

16. In the experience of the group, in the experiences of all, we will find in the coming months, in the coming year, those periods when there will be a greater seeking through these channels.  Will ye be ready to set that as is the standard? What, then, is the standard?  "OTHERS, Lord, not myself but others, that the glory of the Father may be manifested in the earth; and as the day of the Lord approaches that many may be ready and willing to answer, 'Here am I, use me.'  'Here am I, use me.'"

> 
> (Copy was autographed with the inscription, "For [[Gertrude Cayce|Gertrude]]
> Cayce from her third son, Tom."
> 
> Being the Preface to a Volume, Now in Preparation, on the
> Life and Work of Edgar Cayce...
> 
> Published by [[A.R.E. (Association for Research and Enlightenment)|THE ASSOCIATION]] FOR RESEARCH AND ENLIGHTENMENT,
> INCORPORATED, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Virginia
> 
> "There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the
> city of God..."  [[Psalms|Psalms]] 46
> 
> It is not here my [[Purpose|purpose]] to strike on the bandor of time
> more notes than were awarded, in the beginning of my plan,
> these minutia of preface.  I had intended to announce in
> brief the [[Purpose|purpose]] of my efforts, the effect toward which
> sentence and paragraph and chapter are aimed.  But it seems,
> as with all things in which man and his mind properly have no
> business, that I am entangled.  Such simplicities as were
> clear to me in their birth have clouded on the pages where
> they were to mature.  Their dress in words cannot be a
> [[Greece|Grecian]] gown, simple and caught at the middle with a golden
> clasp.  They demand designs with which I am not now, nor ever
> will be, familiar.  Yet in the face of challenge I can but
> cross their desires with language, and hope they will come
> forth fulfilled.
> 
> The story of Edgar Cayce is not the chronicle of a man born,
> appointed to a [[Destiny|destiny]], and fulfilling it.  It is not a
> biography built upon facts, garnished with anecdote, and
> served with similitude and interpretation.  It has more to
> itself than that.  It demands, in its telling, the scope and
> poetry of legend, the depth and preciseness of history, the
> clarity of science, the exactness of mathematics, and the
> [[Visions|vision]] of religion.  Such abilities do not come in the same
> human package.  The mind with which I had hoped to mate the
> matter of this tale has no solution for its meanings, its
> ramifications, its subtleties and myriad implications.  Only
> by the inflation of a vast insolence, and loud singing to
> drown the voice of fear, can this venture hope to pass
> through the dark valley of verb and come out walking.
> 
> There is contained in the life of every man, whatever his
> caste in the system of human intelligence, a mystery as great
> as the universe:  a formula so intricately woven, so
> cunningly patterned, that even his [[Soul|soul]], groping through
> ages of thought, can but feebly guess at the solution.  The
> architecture of his skeleton, the chemistry of his flesh, are
> phenomena which he has never more than vaguely understood.
> Of his mind he has no knowledge at all, except that it
> operates - objectively upon the life which besets him from
> without subjectively upon the death which gathers within.  He
> has no certain knowledge of how to deal with either of these
> exigencies, and in puzzlement he blends them, to his further
> bewilderment.  He labors in the life without him; in order to
> preserve the life within him; yet the execution of this labor
> is the matter upon which the death that grows within him
> feeds.  His existence is a paradox:  he moves inexorably
> toward extinction, laboring that he may preserve himself for
> it.  He works that he may live that he may die.
> 
> To the beast and the flower this is not an irrational
> procedure.  There are other beasts and other flowers,
> descendants of those which return to earth.  Nor does it
> seem unreasonable to the average man that people in [[China|China]]
> occasionally die.  It is when this fact of cessation presents
> itself to the individual that mind fails to follow the
> pathways of matter.  It is something with which reason,
> however fortified by evidence, will have nothing to do.  It
> will sanction all things but its end.  To this it presents
> argument; of this it is afraid.  The first man with a [[Soul|soul]],
> trapped in his cave by fever, or age, balked at it.  To him
> had come a [[Revelation (Book)|revelation]]:  that he was lord of the earth.  He
> would not relinquish his scepter.
> 
> So made for himself, this man in the cave, a comforter.  He
> told himself of another life, beyond the winds and the seas
> and the rivers and the forests:  a life wherein there was
> always harvest, and where age could not enter in.  He told
> it, before he died, to his sons and daughters and wife, and
> said he would meet them there.  They believed him, and they
> were happy.
> 
> We, too, have believed that story, and we have been happy
> because of it.  We have examined the architecture of our
> skeletons and the chemistry of our flesh, and have found no
> answer to them.  We have divided our minds into squares and
> circles and put names upon them, but nothing has come of it.
> We have questioned each other closely about the thoughts that
> have been engendered within us, and have found no cause for
> joy.  In the end we have accepted the word of the witnesses
> of death, who have told us that peace comes to the faces of
> those who are about to travel into nothingness.  With this
> as our guide we have carried our ancestors and our lovers and
> our friends to their graves, and have prayed for them.  We
> have built churches, and taken to ourselves religion.  We
> have made our hope into a belief, and our belief into a
> social structure.  We have taught others to believe as we
> believe, for in numbers there is strength.  We do not wish
> to go into this nothingness alone.
> 
> Yet when the city lights have receded and the doors of our
> churches are closed we stand alone with doubt.  We can gaze
> through telescopes at stars greater than our sun, and
> speculate on other worlds in the space toward which some
> day we will be propelled.  We can see upon the blackboard of
> a schoolroom scrawling numbers which prove that space and
> matter and life and death are one.  We can pass swiftly
> through the air, or over our lands, or through the seas, or
> even underground.  We can receive from the hands of the
> chemist substances which restore our health.  We can hear in
> our homes the voices of men a thousand miles away.  But from
> none of these can we receive surcease from our end, from
> none of these can we gain knowledge to illumine our doubt.
> With this we must grapple alone.
> 
> The facts are few.  At some unspecified time we emerge into a
> world with which we are not familiar, and with which we are
> not equipped to cope.  At another unspecified time we leave
> it.  We are, when we die, somewhat larger in body than when
> we are born, but this has no bearing on our contemplation of
> the two events.  Before birth we had nothing to do with our
> bodies, and after death we have nothing to do with them.
> What, then, are we?  We are apparently our thoughts.  When we
> do not have them we do not exist.  When we have them we are
> alive.  We do not remember having had any before our birth,
> nor for some time thereafter.  As we die they flee from us.
> Yet they are we.  How then can they flee from us?  We must,
> perforce, go with them.  But where?
> 
> They may be, these thoughts, only another of our senses.
> These, we know, die.  They pass away slowly, painlessly,
> leaving us without feeling, without sight, without hearing,
> without taste, without smell.  When we are young they are
> strong and alert.  They pick up the world for us, and bring
> it to our minds.  When their work is done they begin to
> sleep, and then to die.  Our eyes grow dim, our touch is
> muddy, our ears and our taste buds and our noses are
> untrustworthy.  We can no longer smell spring in the air,
> nor feel it against our feet, nor sight it through the bark
> of trees.  We grow old, and only remembrance of things makes
> them real.
> 
> As my fingers strike the keys of the typewriter which is
> translating these thought impulses into words, tiny cells are
> brushed off my finger ends.  These cells, which were a part
> of me, are destroyed.  So soon as they leave me, however,
> trophic nerves, which carry impulses throughout the body,
> send an SOS to headquarters.  Immediately the blood supply
> is marshalled for service, cells are put into swift-flowing
> streams, and dropped into the empty spaces.  The tissue is
> repaired, and I have no knowledge that damage was done.
> 
> If I suffered from anesthetic leprosy this repair would not
> take place.  My trophic nerves would not send any message,
> and my body would be unaware that help was needed.  No cells
> would replace those worn off by the keys, and when I had
> completed the life of Edgar Cayce my fingers would be stubs.
> 
> That is what happens to our senses.  As life proceeds from
> the simplicities of childhood to the complexities of
> adolescence, maturity, and senility, we become confused.  An
> odor that was once the perfume of the roses becomes a snarl
> of memories, a heap of circumstances and happenings in which
> the perfume of roses was in some way concerned.  Our senses,
> besides being duller, are no longer straightforward in their
> accounting to us.  Our eyes no longer see a house in which we
> live.  They see a kaleidoscope of happenings and events and
> people that have had to do with us in that house.  Our taste
> buds no longer tell us that we are drinking Rhine wine.
> They tell us instead that we are sitting on a sidewalk in
> Budapest, watching the Danube and the people who are passing
> by.  The senses have given over to their own death.  They
> are immersed in a pile of memories, recognizing things only
> because of the remembrances these things incur.
> 
> Likewise the mind, struggling to extricate itself from this
> mass of wreckage, becomes confused.  Desperate to escape, it
> denies itself:  it says it has no part in the wreckage, it
> calls what is past the [[Subconscious Mind|subconscious]].  It divides and
> redivides itself, making excuses for its incompetencies,
> tracing its mistakes to prenatal influence and heredity,
> blaming its dullness on environment, seeking to escape
> through a process of mental suasion that depends for its
> rationality upon irrationality.  In the end it comes face
> to face with itself again, having rounded the block in its
> flight and arrived, panting and exhausted, precisely at the
> point where it began.  It is inevitably itself, existing
> without effort, but unsure of its future.  The doubt remains.
> 
> All of these things are known to each of us, and each of us,
> now and again, ponders them.  In our youth we talk of them to
> other persons, until we perceive that these other persons are
> precisely as muddled as we are, and as fearful.  Then we take
> the task within us, and thereafter turn it over and exercise
> it, frequently at first, then with less and less regularity,
> and finally at only those times when the hand of the potter
> seems about the crush us in the mold.
> 
> It was my fortune when, in my now quite faraway youth I was
> putting these things to myself and others, that a listener
> happened in who was not of the common herd.  He seemed,
> though as young as I, to be unworried.  He smiled tolerantly
> at my voiced heresies - wild cries of fear that I tried to
> make into shouts of courage.  To my inchoate philosophies he
> made quiet answer, puncturing their tender shells with barbs
> of what I forthwith considered genius.  I took him to my
> bosom.  Through long nights of the 1926 winter in Lexington,
> Virginia, in our tiny rooms in Lees Dormitory, when we should
> have been preparing lessons for the stern masters of
> Washington and Lee University, we talked of the doubt.
> 
> He said he knew, this bespecaled young man who so willingly
> gave up sleep to instruct me.  I asked him how he knew.  Then
> for days he would not talk to me, and I saw him brooding over
> his books, staring at me occasionally with opaque eyes,
> staring at me occasionally with eyes that shot into me and
> stabbed at my nerves.  Then one night he told me how he knew.
> 
> "My father," he said, "is a psychic medium."
> 
> It is now a long time since that happened, and I do not
> remember much except that we stayed up all night, and that
> at breakfast we were still talking.  I wanted to know, but
> I did not believe.  I was a [[Rome|Roman]] Catholic Irishman from
> Connecticut.  He, [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]], was a Christian Church
> member from Virginia.  There was no valid reason why we
> should like each other, why we should be together.  It was,
> I thought afterward, almost indecent.
> 
> But he said he knew, and he talked of a God Who was merciful,
> Who was understanding, Who saw eye to eye with my blundering
> desires in life.  So we forsook sleep on other nights, and
> talked again, and again.
> 
> He told me of a man, his father, who lay down to sleep and
> spoke with the voice of authority:  who cured the halt and
> the lame and the sick and the blind; who told the story of
> the world before history began; who traced in the minds of
> those who came to ask him, other lives on earth and the
> promise of more lives to be; who spoke of the [[Old Covenant|Old Testament]]
> and the [[New Covenant|New Testament]] as truth; who made heaven and salvation
> a reward for the strong, not a dull fortress in an
> inconceivable land manned by the wimpering and the cowardly
> who had prayed and given alms and fled from battle.
> 
> So in that spring I went with the boy to [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]],
> Virginia, to see his father.  I expected a dark-haired,
> sharp-eyed, stalwart man who walked with gods and spoke in
> the language of metaphysics.  I met a mild, amiable, soft
> spoken man who on the first day changed his cigarette brand
> to suit mine, who allowed me to teach him the intricacies of
> rummy, and who asked me to go fishing with him.  He took me
> down town and bought me a [[Baking Soda|soda]], introduced me to the village
> barber, and played three sets of tennis with me.  I went to
> bed in his house that night with complete peace.  Such a nice
> man, I knew, would not let [[Fallen Angels|the devil]] get me.
> 
> All that, too, seems a long time ago.  He has been my friend
> now for nearly ten years.  I am so conversant with his life
> and with his work that I begin where I began myself, and
> explain it step by step, seems a confusing and arduous task.
> Perhaps I should have done it long ago, before I was so lost
> in the trees that I could not see the forest.  Perhaps I
> should not ever have attempted it.
> 
> Yet there is, in my files, an envelope labeled, "Readings;"
> and there is, in my mind and in my heart, a certain peace.
> These things are due to Edgar Cayce.  Since that day when we
> played rummy and discussed cigarettes and drank [[Baking Soda|soda]] and
> played tennis, no one of the questions which arise in the
> dark hours to plague the minds and hearts of men has troubled
> me.  The soundness of my body, the temper of my mind, the
> quiet of my [[Soul|soul]], are due to him.  He has slept for me many
> times, with the sun across his face, and he has told me who
> I am and what I ought to be, and who I was in the years that
> are history and legend.
> 
> He has informed me of the reasons for my ills and my
> frettings; and given me ointments for their heal.  He has
> calmed me when I was unruly, and praised me when I was meek.
> He has set my feet again and again on the highway of
> humility, and held over and over again that mirror before my
> face in which is reflected the charge of my existence.
> 
> These things he did when asleep.  Waking, he has given me
> roof and sustenence, friendship and company.  We have walked
> together through the canyons of Manhattan and over the sands
> of [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]].  We have whiled away the hours on golf
> courses, and on the shaded porches of his home.  He has
> listened to my troubles, and unburdened himself to me.  There
> is not any man for whom I hold greater love.  He is, in
> brief, my friend.  I believe in him; I believe in his labors;
> I believe in his gifts.
> 
> Therefore this task has come to me.  I had hoped that someone
> more worthy would be appointed.  I know that I am inadequate,
> that never in my life will a more difficult problem be set
> before me.  It deserves better hands.  Yet the time has come
> for its fulfillment, and no other workman is at hand.  I have
> taken it gladly; it is my honor.
> 
> To those who know Edgar Cayce and his work I have this to
> say:  the breadth of the assignment has not escaped me.  I
> know that on any one of the innumerable subjects which have
> coursed through his 25,000 readings a book could be written.
> These books, in time, will be written.  My [[Book of Job|job]] is to break
> the ground, to lay down with as much completeness and
> exactitude as is possible the fundamentals of Edgar Cayce,
> his life, and his work.  Much must be excluded.  Things that
> were momentus in their days of happening seem, in
> perspective, shadowy details that stray into dull byways.
> The outline, massive in years and importance, remains.  It
> is this which I hope to get solidly planted between covers.
> The rest, in due course, will follow.
> 
> To those who come upon Edgar Cayce for the first time let
> this be warning:  it is no tale to take in stride.  Whatever
> else it gives you, it will give you pause.  There is not in
> the chapters which will follow any program of ballyhoo, any
> attempt to sell a product.  This is a document for the
> [[Purpose|purpose]] of record.  Those who would quibble, who would
> point the finger of their intelligence in scorn, who would
> call questions and cry for proof, had best leave off and
> take again to their school books.  Here is a river for those
> who wish to bathe; but those who enter it had best know how
> to swim.
> 
> Thomas Sugrue
> June 15, 1936

> Dear Mrs. [2118]:
> 
> I've wanted to write you ever since receiving yours of the
> 23rd, and I only wish now I knew how to say just how much
> I appreciate your interest...most of all, somehow I feel,
> Mrs. [2118], you have gotten a better concept of what my real
> inner desire is to do and be, than most anyone I know; or at
> least you express it in such a beautiful way that it presents
> to my own concept just what I would like to put into words.
> 
> We had the reply from Mrs. [1267] and I'm hoping, trusting
> and [[Prayer|praying]] with her that the suggestions as given will prove
> to be as valuable to her as such information has been to so
> many hundreds of others.  We sent to you a copy of the
> reading.
> 
> I assure you it has been quite gratifying to us to have so
> many wonderful reports continually.  Very recently there was
> a young lady [852] visiting us who had only a few months ago
> been given up by ten physicians, who said it was impossible
> for her to get well...for they didn't know what was the
> matter with her, and acknowledged it.  Yet she is now in
> almost perfect health, nineteen years old and with all the
> opportunities and enthusiasm that goes with young womanhood,
> with a desire to finish her schooling and to prepare herself
