# TEXT OF READING 900-340 M 32 (Stockbroker, Hebrew)

**Reading [[900-340]]** · Series: [[801-1500 — Psychological and Dream Interpretation Readings|801-1500]] · Year: 1927 · Sex: M

## Text

Date: 9/5/1927  Sex: M  Age: 32  ReadingID: 5543

This psychic reading given by Edgar Cayce at his office, 115 West 35th Street, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 5th day of September, 1927, in accordance with request made by self - Mr. [900].

[Edgar Cayce; [900], Conductor; [[Gladys Davis|Gladys Davis]], Steno. Mrs. Edgar Cayce, Mrs. [136], Mrs. Thos. B. House, L. B. and [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]].]

Time of Reading 
4:00 P. M. Eastern Standard Time.         [[New York City|New York City]], N.Y.

1. [900]:  You will have before you the body [900] and his writing, the book he is seeking to compile.  Book number one, entitled "The Clue" is practically finished.

2. EC:  (Interrupting)  Then should be published!  [Mr. [900] published his booklet in 1928 [1929?] called "Heaven On Earth."  Now out of print - See booklet under Source File Key #900 re 900-340.]

3. [900]:  That's what the [[Dreams|dream]] told me recently.

4. EC:  Then this, as is seen, was given as the command that that as has been compiled is worthy of the attention of so many that are in doubt and are STRUGGLING to see the light. This gives many the perfect understanding of that which they have sought for, but have not been able to put into the WORKABLE form, even for the mind.  Then why delay?  The time is at hand.  The field is right.  The harvest needs to be gathered.  The work is ready.  Then let's be up and doing, for the night cometh when no man can work.  Now, this is not ordinarily understood.  Then, in this case does not mean death.  It does not mean the close of day, but rather that the individual has become so hardened, so engrossed, so hedged about by the various circumstances and conditions, that the individual is incapable of gaining the proper concept and to do the work as should be done for the development of self toward the mark of the higher calling. The work is prepared.  The field is right, the field is ready.  Let's publish same!  GAIN the criticisms, GAIN the plaudits - but do not let same go to the head!  Gain that understanding as comes from accomplishing and doing things, see?

5. (Q)  Now, taking the number of words and exact manner in which this Book One "The Clue" is written, how should I best publish it, or rather to what publisher should I bring it. There are many, but some more in favor of publishing this than others.

(A)  This should be bound in purple or in black buckram, should be an eightmo, should be in sufficient type and margin that questions may be noted in same.  This should be presented either to Schuster or to Doran, or to ANY of such publishers.  These two are well, see?

6. (Q)  Both of these mentioned, or rather either of these - upon reading my manuscript - will accept same for publication?
(A)  Will accept same for publication, with some alterations as will be necessary through the editing of such matter, which will neither add nor take from the matter itself.

7. (Q)  I assume this may mean breaking up into chapters, etc?
(A)  Breaking up into chapters, headings, etc.

8. (Q)  Is there enough material in Book One for a whole book?
(A)  Sufficient if they are given as one.  Meaning this is indicating that others are to follow.  Sufficient matter in Book One for many others to write many VOLUMES, taking this as a basis.

9. (Q)  It will only make a small pamphlet -
(A)  Hundred to hundred and twenty-five pages, yes.  Digest this, and it will make a man much larger than the ordinary man, in mind.

10. (Q)  Now, taking Book Two that I am writing, "Evolution and [[Reincarnation|Reincarnation]]," in -
(A)  (Interrupting)  This will be a good one!

11. (Q)  (Continuing)  In going into the work so deeply I have gotten myself into a little hot water -
(A)  See it boiling up, yes.

12. (Q)  Now let's see - in environment and heredity -
(A)  Much has been given as regarding these various conditions as have been presented to the body, for in acquiring the perfect understanding of hereditary conditions and environnmental conditions, let not hereditary mean only that of the present generation, see?  That is [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the ENTITY]]. We have begun to reason from inside out.  Not from outside in.  Though the illustrations may be presented that are seen from outside in.

In environmental conditions, a great study has been made recently by Brennan concerning this very condition, in which there are some twenty thousand of the illiterates and the ones who have been termed as weak minded that have lived in an environmental condition until the condition itself has presented that as from the scientific standpoint of being the cause of insanity.

Then, in the presentation of hereditary conditions, and of environmental conditions, we find by the comparison of these two, this presents quite a condition to be reckoned with, and - as has been for the ages past, and will be for ages onward - the study of man as to whether environmental or heredity [conditions] produce the greater part in the one development. The answer is the application ever of will in either condition.  Reckoning then as to whether will has been applied in more of the earth experiences, or has that of environmental used, rather than been used by [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the entity]] itself.  Get the point?  HAS [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the entity]] in its experience through its will APPLIED that of will toward the development, or has it allowed itself to be used by the environment and become subject to environment, or has it developed itself through its will towards its own hereditary position - for ALL are the [[Sons of God|children of God]].  How has the development led?

13. (Q)  Now, let's take the animal.  One great belief at the present time is the survival of the fittest -
(A)  Applies in the ANIMAL kingdom - not in man!

14. (Q)  That's a touchy point, and it will take a lot of explanation on my part.
(A)  Let all read history!  Which has survived - the brute strength, or the development towards that of God?  Which survives - the man that studies God and seeks to emulate His forces and powers, or the man that emulates the forces of earth or flesh?  This answers itself!

15. (Q)  Well, now I take it that misapplication of creation, or of the coming into being of environment is correct - that is, the pushing of its way finally into liquid state and then carrying on into the mineral kingdom, vegetable kingdom, and lower animals of this original homogeneity, and then the coordination of its elements to push further into a two dimensional environmental condition that - carrying on in a duplication process - builds up in the lower kingdoms that called heredity.
(A)  Correct.  Yet we find this may be carried to that very same extent, unless there is taken into consideration that as this condition existed, as this evolution began in the mind of the Creator, there then came the point, the place, the beginning, when that as created was GIVEN that as was necessary to make its development by applying these same forces ONE with that Creative Energy.  Hence the next creation.

16. (Q)  I have to study this.
(A)  That is, the kingdoms then being termed in the manner in which the conception may be gained better of this in earth's plane we are dealing with, alone, in this kingdom we find first, as the worlds created - and are still in creation in this heterogeneous mass as is called the outer sphere, or those portions as man looks up to in space, the mists that are gathering - what's the beginning of this?  In this same beginning, so began the earth's sphere.  The earth's sphere, with the first creation in the mind of the Creator, has kept its same Creative Energy, for God is the same yesterday, today and forever, and same in one creation creates that same in the other creation.  One keeps right on through with the other, see?  Now, as this mass has pushed up into that wherein it reverses itself, as it were, making then its own environmental condition, and the survival of the stronger of these came to that as the animal kingdom, see?  Now, as this came into the animal kingdom, then correct is that as is said, "God said, we will make man."  Then man, the creation in itself, that combining all of the forms of creation so far created, that that same force might understand by having passed through that same creation as was necessary to bring up to that dividing point between man and animal and plant, and mineral kingdom - GIVEN then [[Free Will|the will]], and [[Soul|the soul]], that it might make itself One with that Creation.  Now, that will, then, is heredity.  That environment is the evolution.  There you have [[Reincarnation|reincarnation]], there you have evolution, there you have the mineral kingdom, the plant kingdom, the animal kingdom, each developing towards its own source, yet all belonging and becoming one in that force as it develops itself to become one with the Creative Energy, and one with the God.  The one then surviving in the earth, through mineral, through plant kingdom, through the vegetable kingdom, through the animal kingdom, each as the geological survey shows, held its sway in the earth, pass from one into the other; yet man given that to be lord over all, and the ONLY survivor of that creation.

17. We are through for the present.

> character, strength and stability does marriage fail.

> growing thoughts and ideals of home, children, social
> relationship and interesting companionship.  They may tread
> the road of life together, enjoying at every step a broader

> Chapter II
> 
> WE MAY TRAIN THE MIND AGAINST CIRCUMSTANCES AND OBTAIN
> DOMINION FOR SELF OVER ENVIRONMENT.  HAPPINESS IS THE RESULTS
> OF THE ACQUISITION OF BOTH SPIRITUAL AND PHYSICAL VALUES.
> 
> Practical methods and physical values are certainly a
> criterion to be used in the material world.  Yet a man is to
> be judged by his ideas and ideals and motives as well as by
> his actions.  One's material activity and the results thereof
> may or may not betray his mental activity.  There is the
> value one may place upon the mind of a man and its activity
> in terms of thoughts, ideals and motives, and also the value
> one may place upon the worldly wealth, prestige and social
> position he possesses.  The latter physical accomplishments
> should be a reflection of the motives, attitude of mind and
> strength of will of an individual.  Yet many a low minded,
> insincere person attains wealth and prominence; whereas many
> an idealist, however, sincere and high minded, often seems in
> physical application a bungler, at least insofar as the
> attainment of material success is concerned.  The former type
> would be neglecting spiritual values for physical and
> sacrificing thereby everything permanently worth while.  The
> latter type, to say the least, needs to learn a better mode
> of application, of his mental qualities, in order that he
> himself may not lose the benefit of achieving physical values
> and material comfort, nor become misunderstood by those who
> judge, not the quality of mind itself, but judge the mental
> and physical side of man by his application and the material
> results thereof.  That is to say, most of us judge a man by
> the material things he does, says, and, above all,
> accomplishes in terms of material success.  Yet a high minded
> idealist may be a bungler in his manner of speech and
> actions, nor need his qualities of mind mean much to him in
> dollars and cents; whereas a low minded individual may appear
> tactful, smooth, pleasant and materially successful.  That is
> why the low minded individual may so often deceive even the
> discriminating among us, or in common parlance of the street,
> "get away with it."
> 
> We may say that such a high minded individual's ideals do him
> little good in this material world, and we would be justified
> in such a conclusion insofar as his ability to secure
> physical values is concerned.  But while physical comfort,
> independence and leisure are of value to our physical being,
> the spiritual or inner, [[Intuition|intuitive]] and permanent self requires
> the realization of the ideal of peace and love as that of
> value to itself.  It seeks self-expression of the [[Dreams|dreams]] of
> the imagination, and materialization thereof in terms of
> environment.  Just physical comfort that money may but will
> not satisfy its longing for larger self-expression.  When
> thwarted by so-called circumstances, it is the short-sighted
> physical mind that throws up the sponge, never the [[Intuition|intuitive]]
> or subjective mind, that finds power and joy in overcoming
> obstacles.
> 
> There is no question of the greater satisfaction and
> gratification life holds for the highminded individual who
> may, even so, bungle in application as to lose much of the
> physically valuable qualities of life such as money, luxuries
> and social position.  His mind is his castle into which he
> may retreat, and he may refuse entrance thereto to any
> disquieting element, either in the form of gossip or
> discomforting circumstances, etc.  Not that failure to secure
> certain desirable physical values elevates him to the
> position of martyr to his ideals, but that the loss he
> sustains is of less importance to his total spiritual and
> permanent physical, mental and spiritual development, i.e.,
> to his individuality and character, and also to the sum total
> of his happiness.  He gains ground for his permanent, inner,
> mental being in which he may live, even if he loses that
> which is so desirable to his temporary, physical being.  By
> virtue of such a gain for his inner subjective or soul being,
> he becomes able to attune his mind to the higher, unseen or
> physically unobservable forces of life, that bring to his
> consciousness realization of that within himself and
> environment that is the permanent lasting and universal
> element of life.  It raises him to a higher plane of being;
> he becomes part of an advanced race; it yields him a state of
> consciousness that is introspective as well as objective in
> character and induces an emotional state of novelty, peace
> and tranquillity, often described by us as being heavenly.
> 
> Certainly physical comforts, a certain degree of freedom from
> the daily grind of earning bread to eat and clothes to wear,
> and some leisure for congenial social relationship with some
> of our fellow men, are most desirable and not to be
> disregarded.  It is that the desire to secure physical values
> should not eclipse the desire to secure mental and spiritual
> values, that in the last analysis are more permanent and
> lasting in their beneficial effects to ourselves.  Both
> mental quality, motive, attitude of mind and [[Free Will|will power]], or,
> in other words, spiritual values, and able application
> therefore to secure mental satisfaction and also physical
> values are necessary to obtain for the individual the most to
> be obtained from life, either in the acquisition of
> knowledge, or in securing physical comfort and independence.
> It is the way in which we think and the manner in which we
> act to realize and materialize our thoughts and ideals that
> determines whether we find boredom in and from environment,
> or make environment reflect our own growing mental state.  We
> must seek to satisfy both our soul being and physical being
> if the all-important [[Trinity|trinity]] of happiness is to be achieved -
> namely:  Peace, Love and Comfort.
> 
> In short, our life and its happiness is dependent upon our
> imagination, ideals, knowledge and thoughts (i.e. spiritual
> values), as well as upon physical environment or physical
> values.
> 
> We react mentally to outside stimulus as we may choose to
> react.  We voluntarily permit environment to stimulate our
> emotions.  A slimy mud pond may cause us to be repelled.  We
> would be unhappy in such an environment.  Yet by thinking of
> the beauty of the lily that takes root in mud, and that
> pushes its tender and harmoniously tinted petals to the
> surface of the slimy pond, we may change our whole emotional
> reaction.  We may seek the happiness to be gained from
> spiritual values, which we may obtain through so training the
> mind.  By applying ourselves to beautify or change the slimy
> mud environment to a spot of beauty we seek, in addition to
> spiritual values, physical values, both of which
> cooperatively produce in our life a balanced state of mental
> peace that permits us to enjoy to the utmost the fashioning
> of new and greater thoughts that are the outgrowth of a
> growing and expanding mind, unhampered by severe emotional
> reactions that an individual experiences, when environment,
> rather than his own will, determines the course of his
> imagination.  The novelty and joy of greater materialization
> and realization of these growing ideas of an expanding mind,
> whether related to our business, home, art, social
> relationships, or what not, is unending.
> 
> Living in mind first, and in environment second, is achieving
> for self a guarantee against boredom and an insurance of
> life-long happiness.  Both spiritual and material values are
> worth while, yet to achieve physical values of comfort and
> luxury either at the expense of, or to the neglect of, the
> spiritual, is to kick the strongest prop from under our
> happiness.
> 
> The fact that some do not seek to express themselves in terms
> of love, sympathy and gratitude need not stop us from seeking
> to bring out the best in others and in ourselves, and to
> bring it out inspires its own reward in self-realization of
> that which we are making of ourselves, which we find
> objectified in the environment we strive to change to reflect
> our own individuality.  Our constructive influence upon and
> in the life of others becomes a source of satisfaction,
> irrespective of others' reaction thereto.  Epictetus long ago
> pointed out that criticism and that which others say about
> us, or do to us, cannot hurt us if we do not choose to let
> our mind dwell upon that said to or of us, or upon that done
> to us.  Shutting out the undesirable is a sign of
> self-control and of the ability to control environment rather
> than be controlled by it.  Intuitively we seek to do just
> that.
> 
> The difficulty lies in our not understanding the value to our
> happiness and life in seeking to satisfy the inner, [[Intuition|intuitive]]
> longing.  Unconsciously we feel the lack of such
> gratification in the lack of peace, or turbulent and boring
> mental condition we experience when we seek purely physical
> values, or when we permit environment, the house we live in,
> money we have to spend, luxuries we possess, to govern our
> emotional reaction.  By training the mind we may be happy in
> every or any environment, while striving to improve the
> latter in order to secure greater mental satisfaction as well
> as physical comfort for ourselves.  Happiness is an emotional
> reaction, under our control or not, according to whether we
> choose to use our volition to mentally govern our emotions or
> to yield this control to become emotionally subjected to
> conditions outside of ourselves.  If we think as environment
> leads us to, then our imagination becomes enslaved by outside
> conditions - i.e., by environment.  It becomes as a piece of
> paper blown hither and thither by the wind, and we become
> tormented by the lash of one's own emotions.  If we think as
> we want to think, irrespective of outside conditions, then we
> are [[Jesus Christ|the master]] of our life, of our mind, and we become immune
> to bad environment and adverse circumstances.  We make within
> our mind our own environment and circumstances, striving ever
> to change outside conditions to likewise reflect our
> dominion.  We seek to create of matter, (a home, house,
> school, office, church or what not), an objectified image of
> that which we ourselves are in thought, ideal, imagination
> and thought activity.  We attain a kingly power over that
> which life is in and within ourselves, and live to awaken to
> the fact that in a lower form it is the same thing outside of
> ourselves, such that self-dominion means universal control
> for self, a power, a glory and a kingdom to which only the
> inner subjective self may fully awaken, when finally freed
> from the limitations of the objective, physical self.
> 
> Yet self-control means much to the cooperative subjective and
> physical being - i.e., to man.  Lucretius, for example, would
> not even let his mind dwell upon the sensation of pain, when
> his master broke his leg.  He remained mentally happy.  In
> the loss of use of his leg, he sustained loss of something
> physically valuable nevertheless.  If one may create in mind,
> his own condition of happiness, and at the same time apply
> himself to obtain greater physical comfort, then is he
> seeking both spiritual and physical values, and provided he
> does not destroy the opportunity of any other individual to
> do likewise he must become blessed in that peace that
> surpasses all understanding, and that ever seeks to aid and
> inspire although never compel another to seek like happiness
> in a like manner.
> 
> By seeking comfort in comparing our fortune or misfortune
> with another's fortune or misfortune, we seek to make the
> life of another yield an emotional reaction that induces
> envy, and throws the responsibility for our happiness upon
> some condition outside of ourselves - i.e., upon environment
> - rather than where it belongs, namely, upon our own mental
> being, which is dependent for its ability to bring us joy or
> sorrow upon our own control of our own elements of mind.
> 
> Not what we have compared to what another has, but what we
> mentally have in thought and imagination and what we may
> achieve in materialization of same to rejoice in the creation
> of OUR OWN handiwork, as well as secure our physical
> comforts, determines the cooperative happiness of our
> intuitive, spiritual and sensuous physical being.  We
> physically cry out for peace when we suffer the anguish of a
> life based upon physical values alone, yet do not
> sufficiently understand our soul being and its requirements
> as to bring that for which we long and which we call peace.
> 
> Life is big and full of meaning when one uses environment to
> make it reflect one's ever-growing and expanding
> conceptualizations.  Life is small, petty and purely material
> when one seeks in and of environment gratification of some
> new whim or desire.  In the former case we rise up in our
> manhood to work out of environment that which we want it to
> reflect of our own individuality.  In the latter case we but
> seek to have environment do something to our emotions - i.e.,
> furnish us with a thrill.  The senses tire.  The habit of
> self-dominion becomes established in and for ourselves.  We
> become slaves to limitations, trials, tribulations and whims
> of the flesh.  We become as a cloud before the wind-driven
> hither and thither - achieve a purposeless, confused life of
> trouble, mental anguish and disillusionment.
> 
> The soul of our intuitive self, on the other hand, knows not
> the sensation of tiredness.  The dominion over ourselves to
> the subjective self means dominion over environment.  We may
> think as we determine to think, irrespective of environment,
> and we act according to our ideas, striving to materialize
> and realize same in terms of environment.
> 
> The joy of working towards that goal involves a process
> filled with novelty.  As we apply ourselves, we grow in mind,
> in thought and ideal, and thus have ever the more to realize
> and materialize.  We have both in the spiritual environment
> of our mind, with its dreams, ideas, etc., and in the
> physical environment our active application creates of
> matter, the dual nature of life and the necessity of taking
> both into consideration.  Indeed, thoughts are deeds, and we
> live in them, and they may become [[Miracles of Jesus|miracles]] or crimes.  We
> live in them mentally first and suffer the consequence or
> penalty to or enjoy the greater development of our inner
> subjective soul being, according to whether our thoughts and
> ideals are conducive to mental growth, self control and
> power, or whether they are conducive to mental degeneration
> and loss of control of self, by self, to a material
> environment, i.e., that which we hear and see others say or
> do etc., irregardless of the social or physical position of
> authority occupied by those others.  Afterwards, we enact our
> thoughts and live in them in the form they take as deeds, and
> then suffer the penalty of breaking a civil or physical law,
> or of enjoying the fruits of material application of
> so-called "good" rather than so-called "bad" thoughts.
> First, we pay the penalty spiritually for so-called evil
> thoughts, or reap spiritual reward for so-called good
> thoughts.  After physical application of the latter to
> change them into deeds, we pay the penalty of breaking the
> civil law, or reap the reward to be obtained for good deeds
> that are but good thoughts put into action.  As pointed out
> before, where hypocritical individuals make their deeds seem
> good to avoid the penalty of the civil law, the evil thoughts
> and motives however covered up even in mind, exact
> nevertheless payment of the penalty for breaking spiritual
> law which governs spiritual values and that side of our
> nature that is permanent and therefore real, i.e., not that
> side of our nature that has to do alone with the present
> aspect of what we may come to understand to be the
> everpresent continuous process of life.
> 
> We may think our hovel beautiful and act accordingly and thus
> be happy in our hovel.  Fooling ourselves?  Not at all.
> Beauty is a quality we project out of our mind, i.e., it is
> an idea that we impose upon our surroundings, not something
> created in and for us by our surroundings - unless we choose
> to let our surroundings cause us to form ideas.  We form our
> ideas as we choose - voluntarily, as we have already shown.
> Environment but forms a means for the projection of our
> ideas, whether of beauty, ugliness or what not.  I may enjoy
> the beauty of a hovel and find happiness in working out my
> own ideas therein.  I may be physically more comfortable in
> a mansion and to better advantage work out my ideas of beauty
> in such an abode, but it is only a greater degree of
> happiness I gain in the mansion.
> 
> Both physical and spiritual values must be considered in the
> all-important search for happiness.  One needs both, and
> while the lack of spiritual values will not disqualify the
> comfort the physical side of our natures may enjoy, there is
> within human nature that which requires more than just the
> things wealth will buy, whether food to eat, clothes to wear,
> a house to live in, or adornments and luxuries of all
> descriptions.
> 
> Nor does the spiritual quality of love make itself felt in
> our complex nature unless we express it in terms of service
> in and for others.  Social position attains its full meaning
> and effect upon us only when so founded.  In itself social
> prestige is empty in its spiritual significance as are all
> other purely material qualities and values.  On the other
> hand, the lack of physical values (material comfort and
> luxury, etc.) does not disqualify the mental process of
> forming happy thought and of our dwelling therein.  Lack of
> physical comfort does not do away with the joy, the unending
> process of self-expression in any environment brings.
> [[Milton Harrison|Milton]] wrote his "Paradise Lost" in a garret, and enjoyed so
> expressing himself, and so, in a lesser emphasized degree,
> with all of us.  Life is the stuff that dreams are made of -
> anticipation of the future is as much a part of our life and
> happiness as is materialization thereof a part of our life.
> 
> Physical values seem the most important to our physical mind,
> and indeed comfort and luxury is dear to the human heart.
> Yet greater happiness is found in spiritual values that
> become part of our own permanent nature, character or
> individuality than in physical values that but yield us
> passing comfort.  Yet in cooperative realization of both
> physical and spiritual values is complete happiness for man
> assured.
> 
> Life, then, for many of us seems an unconscious struggle
> between that which we physically know ourselves to be in the
> form of sensations and desires, and that which we internally
> and intuitively feel ourselves to be in soul and spirit.
> Between the longing for excitement by the former and peace
> for the latter, we seem to vacillate.  In sorrow or trouble
> we cry for peace, where there is no peace except for those of
> us who understand sufficiently the nature of life and its
> [[Purpose|purpose]] as to be able to comprehend the nature of both joy
> and sorrow and its significant relationship to our process of
> experiencing, the latter of which in this world is of a
> physical kind.  In other words, only in understanding our own
> being, physical and spiritual, do we free ourselves from the
> limitations of the physical mind and from physical kind of
> experience alone, breaking down the barriers that often form
> the dividing line between that which produces joy in our life
> and that which produces sorrow.  With the mental smashing of
> these barriers comes a broader [[Visions|vision]] and an insight that
> makes life whole, purposeful and eternal.
> 
> It is by raising our mental being to the realization of our
> inner soul self and its [[Purpose|purpose]], as well as our application
> to accomplish the latter, that we find peace and happiness.
> This inner self, being subjugated to our external physical
> self in this external physical world, the purpose and desire
> of the inner, intuitive self is likely to be neglected or
> sacrificed for the desires of the flesh.  It is to enable the
> reader to gain some understanding and self-realization of the
> inner, subjective or soul being and its wider universal
> relationships in a permanent world, as well as the purpose it
> seeks in an externalized flesh formation (i.e., as man), in
> this temporary physical world, that this book is written.
> Thus we may gain a greater appreciation of the spiritual side
> of life and its requirements, even while viewing it from our
> own physical side, and also obtain the inspiration, power and
> glory to be derived from such an insight, and from such a
> quickened mental being.
>

> Chapter III
> 
> THREE DIFFERENTIATIONS BETWEEN THAT WHICH WE ARE IN PHYSICAL
> BEING AND THAT WHICH WE ARE IN INTERNAL SOUL OR SUBJECTIVE
> BEING.
> 
> Such an awakening or realization of life's physical and
> spiritual values and purpose naturally requires a deeper
> understanding of our own inner, spiritual self and its
> requirements than is ordinarily possessed by the layman.
> Beginning with a consideration of mind, we may seek therein
> the key to our own soul, and the higher values it seeks in
> life.
> 
> Formerly the dual nature of man's mind was accepted only as a
> working hypothesis.  Psychology during the past fifty years
> has made such progress in the study of the mind and its
> phenomena as to accept this proposition to be an existing
> fact.
> 
> Psychologically we are aware of ourselves possessing two
> definite and distinct forms of mind.  One we call the
> physical form that cognizes environment by virtue of the
> functioning of the sensory system and brain forces (i.e., by
> the process of seeing, hearing, feeling, tasting and
> smelling), the other form being what is called the
> [[Subconscious Mind|subconscious mind]] that takes cognizance of environment by
> means of intuition (i.e., by means of an organism that
> possesses the ability of awareness or consciousness without
> the use of physical senses).
> 
> Because it is an externalized form of mind operating by
> virtue of the functioning of the brain and nerve organism,
> and because it is thus able to sensuously cognize an
> externalized or objectified world of matter, the physical
> mind is often called the externalized or "objective" form of
> mind.  In contrast to this, the [[Subconscious Mind|subconscious]], being a form of
> mind capable of cognizing from its own internal viewpoint the
> internal or subjective side of life, is often called the
> internal or intuitive or "subjective" form of mind.  We shall
> understand further along what is meant by the externalized
> side of material life that the objective mind cognizes, and
> the internal side of life that the subjective mind cognizes.
> 
> Now, while these forms of mind are two different aspects of
> our one mental being, they are, nevertheless, entirely
> separate from one another insofar as functioning, attributes,
> and powers are concerned.  Each is capable under certain
> conditions and limitations of independent action, just, as we
> have already shown, each seeks its own values in environment
> and life.
> 
> Hudson in his "Law of Psychic Phenomena" points out very
> specifically three distinct differentiations between our
> inner subjective self and external physical self, which
> latter is really the result of the cooperative operation of
> both forms of mind or being.  We call the result of this
> cooperative operation, our physical or normal self, because
> in this world or in the material environment the externalized
> or physical side of our mind is in control.  The subjective
> or internal side cooperates with the physical, taking the
> direction suggested to it by the physical, and manifests
> through the latter in the form of habit or unconscious action
> just the kind of direction the objective mind has given it.
> We shall return to this again.
> 
> The first of the three differentiations between the objective
> and subjective forms of mind or of our being, according to
> Hudson, is:  "The objective mind, or let us say, the man in
> his normal condition, is not controllable, against reason,
> positive knowledge, or the evidence of his senses, by the
> suggestions of another.  The subjective mind is unqualifiedly
> and constantly amenable to the power of suggestion."
> 
> Hudson illustrates this first differentiation between our two
> aspects of mind by pointing out the example of the individual
> under control of the subjective mind during a period in which
> the physical or objective mind is held in abeyance.  Such a
> person may be called in trance state or in the hypnotized
> state, and while in this state, under control of the
> subjective mind, upon being told by another that he is a dog
> will instantly accept the suggestion, and, to the limit of
> physical possibility act the part suggested.  If he is told
> that he is the president of the United States, he will act
> the part with wonderful fidelity to life.  If the presence of
> devils is suggested, his terror will be instant, and painful
> to behold.  It he is told that he is in the presence of
> [[Angels|angels]] he will be profoundly moved to acts of devotion.  If
> told that he is in a high fever, his pulse will become rapid,
> his face flushed, and his temperature increased.  "In short,"
> says Hudson, "he may be made to see, hear, feel, smell, or
> taste anything, in obedience to suggestion.  He may be raised
> to the highest degree of mental or physical exaltation by the
> same power, or be plunged by it into the lethargic or
> cataleptic condition simulating death."
> 
> Hudson further points out, however, that one's own objective
> mind possesses power of suggestion to which one's own
> subjective mind is amenable even as opposed to the suggestion
> of another.  Choosing examples from the "Law of Psychic
> Phenomena" we cite the fact that a person may not be
> hypnotized or, in other words, have his physical or objective
> kind of mind subjugated against his will.  The suggestion
> made by an individual to the individual's own subjective mind
> of not desiring to be put into the trance or hypnotic
> condition is sufficient to prevent the suggestion from
> another, or from the hypnotist, taking effect.
> 
> We further quote from the book.  "Again, if the subject
> submits to be hypnotized, but resolves beforehand that he
> will not submit to certain anticipated experiments, the
> experiments are sure to fail.  One of the finest hypnotic
> subjects known to the writer would never allow himself to be
> placed in a position before a company which he would shrink
> from in his normal condition.  He was possessed of a
> remarkable dignity of character, and was highly sensitive to
> ridicule; and this sensitiveness stepped into his defence,
> and rendered abortive every attempt to cause him to place
> himself in a ridiculous attitude.  Again, if a hypnotic
> subject is conscientiously opposed to the use of strong
> drink, no amount of persuasion on the part of the operator
> can induce him to violate his settled principles.  And so on,
> through all the varying phases of hypnotic phenomena,
> autosuggestion plays its subtle role, often confounding the
> operator by resistance where he expected passive obedience.
> It does not militate against the force of the rule that
> suggestion is the all-controlling power which moves the
> subjective mind.  On the contrary, it confirms it,
> demonstrates its never-failing accuracy.  It shows, however,
> that the stronger suggestion must always prevail.  It
> demonstrates, moreover, that the hypnotic subject is not the
> passive, unreasoning, and irresponsible automaton which
> hypnotists, ancient and modern, have believed him to be."
> 
> We see then that our subjective being is under control of the
> suggestion and direction given it by our own objective or
> physical being, or by the objective and physical mind of
> another, depending upon whether the suggestion given by our
> own physical self, or that given by another physical mind is
> the stronger.  In other words, if what people do or say to or
> of us determines the course of our thinking, our opinions,
> judgment, attitude of mind and actions, then the inner
> subjective self will become likewise subservient to [[Free Will|the will]]
> and suggestions of others.  If we let environment determine
> our thoughts and mental being, then the subjective side of
> our mind will become likewise dependent upon outside
> dominion.  If we are sufficiently strong minded to control
> the course of our imagination and to admit into our mental
> being what we choose to admit, either in the form of that
> spoken by or enacted by another, or in the form of reaction
> to environment, then our subjective being takes its direction
> first from our own will in any matter.  Our own physical
> thoughts, actions or reactions govern what we subjectively
> become and enact, rather than those of any one else.  We
> become able to control the elements of our own physical and
> soul being - a significant fact, the importance of which must
> already be sensed by the reader.  Let us not, however, plunge
> too deeply into this at the present time.
> 
> It is of course true that our personality, character and
> habits are a reflection of past subjective development,
> achieved under control of the objective form of mind.  The
> latter control, no doubt, consisted of that given by our own
> physical, and also that furnished by other physical minds.
> While I am part and parcel that which I make myself to
> become, it is also true, as a wise poet once said, that I am
> part of everything and everybody I have met.
> 
> This is not at all contradictory to our mental control, as
> exemplified in the first chapter.  First and foremost our
> being is dependent upon the direction given it by our own
> objective mind, and we are subjected to direction from
> another only to the extent we may choose to be so directed -
> unless our own objective mind or, more correctly, [[Free Will|will power]]
> is so weak as to permit that of another individual to control
> our subjective mind or being irrespective of our own desire
> in the matter.  The important point here is that in the
> material environment the physical mind is in control, and the
> subjective, inner aspect of mind must do as directed by the
> objective mind, whether of one's own sensuous mind, or that
> of another individual.  Whether our own or another's
> objective mind does the controlling depends firstly upon our
> will and desire as to whether we choose to let another take
> control, and secondly upon whether we are mentally strong
> enough to prevent such outside control, even if we do not
> desire it.  When the objective mind or will becomes so
> weakened by lack of self-control as to become subjected to
> outside influence as not to be able to use its volition in
> such matters, even to the slightest degree, the subjective
> mind, pushed hither and thither by whatever objectively
> controlling influence happens to be in the saddle at the
> time, will manifest in cooperation with the weak physical
> mind in such an erratic manner as to demonstrate what we
> call insanity.
> 
> The subjective being is a great, powerful factor called soul,
> needing the development it may obtain in an objective or
> material environment, that requires a strong, controlling
> objective mind to safely keep it from detrimental conditions
> while evolving in this earthly experience.
> 
> The second differentiation between the two forms of mind is
> as follows:
> 
> The objective mind is capable of reasoning by all methods -
> inductive, deductive, analytic and synthetic, whereas the
> subjective is incapable of inductive reasoning.
> 
> Again we choose an example from Hudson's book to illustrate
> the deductive reasoning of the subjective mind.  This example
> refers to an individual who has permitted his physical
> faculties of mind to become so deeply subjugated as to enable
> the subjective mind to take control - i.e., to an individual
> in the hypnotized or trance state.  We must remember our
> first proposition while reading this example - namely that
> such an entranced individual is absolutely subjected to
> obedience to the objective mind of the individual who is
> doing the suggesting.  This is because the hypnotized
> individual first voluntarily placed himself under the control
> of the other individual - i.e., suggested to his own
> [[Subconscious Mind|subconscious]] that it take orders from the other individual
> (the so-called hypnotist), rather than from his own physical
> self.  If he is a strong-willed individual and has determined
> not to do certain things while in the hypnotized or entranced
> state, then his subjective mind while under control of the
> other person's objective mind, and while he will say, think
> and act according to the other's direction, he nevertheless
> will not say, think or act against his own previously
> suggested qualifications as regards certain things he did not
> want to do while in the entranced state.  He will, while
> under subjective control, do everything and see everything
> and be everything the other one doing the suggesting (or the
> hypnotist), tells him to do, see, or be except that which he
> himself has previously determined he would not do, see or be,
> even while in the hypnotic state.  If he has not objectively
> made any such provisions prior to becoming entranced, or if
> he is too weak minded, then while under the control of the
> subjective instead of objective aspect of mind, he will be
> completely and absolutely amenable to the suggestions and
> directions of the one individual doing the suggesting or
> directing - or to the hypnotist.  The latter will be the one
> - possibly the only one - whom the entranced individual will
> obey, because that is the one he has objectively instructed
> (so to speak), his subjective mind to become subjected to.
> Thus he voluntarily subjugates his physical faculties and
> puts his subjective being under control of the objective mind
> of another individual.
> 
> Remembering this first proposition, then, we proceed to an
> example of our second proposition - i.e., that the subjective
> reasons deductively:
> 
> "The writer once saw Professor Carpenter, of Boston, place a
> young gentleman in the hypnotic state at a private gathering
> in the city of Washington.  The company was composed of
> highly cultivated ladies and gentlemen of all shades of
> religious belief; and the young man himself - who will be
> designated as C - was a cultured gentleman, possessed a
> decided taste for philosophical studies, and was a graduate
> of a leading college.  In his normal condition he was liberal
> in his views on religious subjects, and, though always
> unprejudiced and open to conviction, was a decided unbeliever
> in modern spiritism.  Knowing his love of the classics and
> his familiarity with the works of the [[Greece|Greek]] philosophers, the
> professor asked him how he should like to have a personal
> interview with Socrates.
> 
> "'I should esteem it a great privilege, if Socrates were
> alive,' answered C.
> 
> "'It is true that Socrates is dead,' replied the professor;
> 'but I can invoke his spirit and introduce you to him.  There
> he stands now,' exclaimed the professor, pointing towards a
> corner of the room.
> 
> "C looked in the direction indicated, and at once arose, with
> a look of the most reverential awe depicted on his
> countenance.  The professor went through the ceremonial of a
> formal presentation, and C, almost speechless with
> embarrassment, bowed with the most profound reverence, and
> offered the supposed spirit a chair.  Upon being assured by
> the professor that Socrates was willing and anxious to answer
> any question that might be put to him, C at once began a
> series of questions, hesitatingly and with evident
> embarrassment at first; but, gathering courage as he
> proceeded, he catechized the [[Greece|Greek]] philosopher for over two
> hours, interpreting the answers to the professor as he
> received them.  His questions embraced the whole cosmogony of
> the universe and a wide range of spiritual philosophy.  They
> were remarkable for their pertinency, and the answers were no
> less remarkable for their clear-cut and sententious
> character, and were couched in the most elegant and lofty
> diction, such as Socrates himself might be supposed to
> employ.  But the most remarkable of all was the wonderful
> system of spiritual philosophy evolved.  It was so clear, so
> plausible, and so perfectly consistent with itself and the
> known laws of Nature that the company sat spell-bound through
> it all, each one almost persuaded, for the time being, that
> he was listening to a voice from the other world."
> 
> The third difference between the two forms of mind or of our
