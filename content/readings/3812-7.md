# TEXT OF READING 3812-7

**Reading [[3812-7]]** · Series: 3001-5747 · Year: 1931 · Sex: F

## Text

Date: 4/10/1931  Sex: F  ReadingID: 11397

This Psychic Reading given by Edgar Cayce at his office, 115 West 35th Street, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 10th day of April, 1931, in accordance with request made by those present.

[Edgar Cayce; [[Gertrude Cayce|Gertrude Cayce]], Conductor; [[Gladys Davis|Gladys Davis]], Steno. A. C. Preston, O. H. Snyder, Gray Salter, [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn]] and L. B. Cayce.]

Time of Reading 
3:00 P. M. Eastern Standard Time.

1. GC:  You will have before you the location known as White Horse Hill on Broad Bay, Princess Anne Co., Va., especially that part of it covered in explorations today by A. C. Preston, O. H. Snyder and Gray Salter, present in this room. Are the locations made this morning with the instrument, which we numbered one, two and three, correct?  You will answer the questions which we will ask you regarding these.

2. EC:  Yes, we have those locations as made today in that vicinity of Broad Bay, Princess Anne County, known as White Horse Hill - this we have had before.

3. As is indicated by the indicator, or finder, these are SPECIFIC locations - though not ALL that may be found in the immediate vicinity.  That as called One, nearest the tree from which the indications were to be taken, this the deeper - yet one of the most important.  While its outline is not as definite as number Two, that toward east or south and east, this - as we find - in ONE - would be that to be indicated or re-checked again.  In EACH location the emanations are so different.  In Three we find more decided, yet a much smaller area.  This in the south and toward the west.  This, as we find, should also be included.  Do not, in the explorations, so handle that to be indicated or found as to RE-handle in the various places.  Begin first by re-checking the area. Outline these in a DEFINITE manner.  Have sufficient either of those to REMOVE same, by a rotation - that is, getting it out of the way - or use some piling.  In the first, we will find nearly twenty to twenty-five feet.  In the second, from eighteen to twenty feet.  In the third, we would find some sixteen to nineteen feet.  Ready for questions.

4. (Q)  Does that mean deep straight down or back in the hill?
(A)  Either way will be near the same.

5. (Q)  Would the bottom of that take it down under water?
(A)  To water LEVEL in some of them.

6. (Q)  Which should be gone after first?
(A)  That as indicated by the re-check of the most DEFINITE point - see?

7. (Q)  Is this hickory tree the right tree to start from?
(A)  It's better to start from the location than from the tree!  The outline that is made by the indicator, THERE start - see?  Outline same.

8. (Q)  Give specific instructions to recover it.
(A)  As given.  Either use those of piling or make the area sufficient that this will not have to be re-handled - see?

9. (Q)  Will there be any difficulties encountered in digging there, from any sources?
(A)  We do not find it so, provided there are those precautions taken as natural and incident to such explorations.

10. (Q)  Any other information that will be helpful at this time, or any warnings?
(A)  CHECK first - RE-check, see?  Follow that one that is the most DEFINITE outline, see?  Outline the area.  Then give self sufficient of an area to remove covering - see?

11. We are through for the present.

Copy to A. C. Preston

> To quote the book the phenomenon of dowsing is; "when certain
> sensitive individuals held between their fingers a flexible
> Y shaped branch, with no intention of bending it, twisting it
> or moving it, the branch will, under certain conditions, turn
> downward."  This turning downward is independent of [[Free Will|the will]]
> of the operator and sometimes is of extreme force.  There are
> many types of dowsers and their proficiency varies greatly
> as Roberts makes clear; that is some are sensitive to water,
> some to minerals, some to both etc.  There is reason to
> believe that many individuals are capable of developing
> dowsing ability.
> 
> That dowsing enjoys a better reputation in other countries
> than the U.S. is shown by the fact that in 1931 the
> Government of [[England|British]] Columbia employed and official
> water-diviner, one Evelyn Penrose, who obtained remarkable
> success and whose map dowsing later coincided very well with
> Henry Gross's in the location of water on Bermuda.  That
> dowsing ability can be developed, and the results obtained by
> one excellent dowser, Henry Gross, comprises the bulk of the
> book.  Roberts first met Henry in Maine and many accounts
> of finding water in this area are given.  Probably the most
> remarkable results to emerge from Henry Gross's experiments
> were that he learned he could question the dowsing rod and
> obtain a yes or no answer depending on whether the rod dipped
> or not in answer to a question, and that dowsing could be
> done from a distance by using maps or photographs.  The best
> illustration of this was the location of three "domes" of
> water in Bermuda by dowsing from a map of Bermuda; location
> and depths were given.  Then Henry Gross went to Bermuda
> and checked the location on the spot, again giving exact
> location, depth and flow of water.  Drilling in these
> locations confirmed the predictions of Henry's rod and water
> was located at the spot and depth indicated; all this in
> spite of the pronouncements of geologists that there was
> no fresh water on Bermuda.
> 
> The whole book contains abundant references, is spiced with
> an excellent sense of humor and makes extremely interesting
> reading.

> Dear Mr. Roberts and Mr. Gross:
> 
> I appreciate your note of the 17th about the M scope.  I
> have some literature from several concerns, the best known
> of which are Fisher and Goldak of Cal.  They each make two or
> three models of the same type locator.  One is similar to an
> army mine detector and has a range of two or three ft.  It
> will detect small objects such as bullets or coins a few
> inches under the surface.  Then there is the heavy duty model
> or M scope (which will not pick up individual coins) but will
> detect larger objects as cans, chests, sheets of metal, water
> pipes, layers of ore etc. at greater depths.  Also there is
> a light weight model of the M scope with very nearly the same
> range in a less deluxe case.
> 
> Though I am not an expert on metal detectors I know enough
> electrical engineering to understand their working principles
> and limitations.  They consist of a transmitter and receiver
> mounted perpendicular to each other on carrying handles.
> This forms a sort of balanced bridge affair such that if
> the ground over which the locator is carried is homogeneous
> no sound will be heard in the receiver, or no deflection
> indicated on a meter.  However, if the locator is passed
> over ground containing a metal object such as chest of
> metal, water pipe, etc. the transmitted electrical field
> is distorted by the difference in conductivity between the
> metallic object and the surrounding ground, and a sound is
> heard in receiver or a deflection noted on a meter.  These
> detectors will work over fresh water but NOT over salt water
> since it is such an excellent conductor there is no way of
> distinguishing between the salt water and the metal object.
> 
> Now the Town of Va. Beach had a heavy duty M scope a couple
> of years ago which they used to make a survey of the water
> pipes in the area.  The town engineer at that time was a good
> friend of mine and we did a lot of experimenting with the
> device.  Needless to say we covered White Hill with it and
> found only some tin cans left by campers.  We went so far
> as to bury an old car engine block (about size of object we
> expected to find) in sand at different depths.  The deepest
> the locator would detect it was about seven feet.
> 
> I have seen other similar detectors work and have letters
> from purchasers of several different makes.  I have some
> lengthy correspondence from a man in Fla. who owns and has
> used both the Fisher and Goldak instruments.  His findings
> agree with mine that the maximum range of these devices on
> a metal object about two ft. by three ft. is around seven to
> eight ft.  In addition I have secured the performance curves
> of these locators from the manufacturers (these are curves
> showing the size of object being located plotted against the
> depth of object in feet)  These curves are linear for only
> a short distance; then, they swing up sharply in a parabola
> which means that to pick up anything at twenty to twenty-five
> ft. it would have to be as big as a house; so, in spite of
> claims of a 27 to 30 ft. range the best these instruments
> will do according to the most optimistic performance curves
> of the manufacturer on an object two ft. by three ft. on a
> side is around eight to ten ft., and in actual practice I
> believe it is somewhat less, although they would probably
> pick up an automobile or a body of ore at fifteen or twenty
> ft.
> 
> Now the VEPCO (where I work) has several of the light weight
> models which they use to locate gas mains.  By experiment I
> have found these able to detect a two ft. by three ft. metal
> object at six to seven ft. deep, and we will have one of
> these available when Henry comes down.
> 
> I have gone into a good bit of detail to show you we have
> not neglected the possibility of using metal locators.  It
> was because of their limitations I became so interested in
> Henry's ability (after reading your book) as he didn't seem
> to be limited by depth.  Also these boxes or chests may be at
> or near water level (probably salt water) and this would foul
> up a metal detector.
>

> arrival.
>

> 2. We are taking a few days off work the end of that week to
> get pumps, hose, beat and all equipment ready to go.
> 3. A metal detector will be available, though I haven't much
> hopes of its working unless the cache is above salt water
> level and we can get within six to eight ft. of it.
> 4. We have secured some steel manhole forms to be used as
> piling in case of digging.
> 
> If Henry can pinpoint the object accurately we plan to remove
> as much of the surface sand as possible with the pumps and
> then dig the remaining distance using the steel piling.
> Since the area is all sand and on the side of a hill we hope
> to accomplish this while Henry is here; it depends on exact
> location and depth.
