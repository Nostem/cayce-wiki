## Reports

> [!quote]- Reports & Follow-up
> R1. 11/62 Letter from HLC to Dr. W. H. [[Wesley Ketchum|Ketchum]]:  "...Enclosed
> is a copy of a diary written by my Grandfather [of EC
> readings given] between October 22 and November, 1911..."
> serving as a stimulus for memory recollection by [[Wesley Ketchum|Dr. Ketchum]].
> 
> [[Wesley Ketchum|Dr. Ketchum]] begins to speak:
> 
> You know, that fellow Cayce - well, - you just didn't
> know where he was going to hit you, that's all!  He'd
> come up with the darndest things after he'd get under
> one of these hypnotic spells.  We'd ask him questions,
> sometimes; sometimes we wouldn't.
> 
> Case of (Boyetna?) [pellagra].  [5778] case diagnosed by
> Cayce as a case of malnutrition.  I was called to see this
> boy - not a "boy", really, he was a man.  He hadn't been
> treated by any other doctor, but he was still sticking by
> his work.  He was doing shovel work in a pit for a brick firm
> by the name of Dalton which employed about 400 men.  ...Was
> buried in the ground and they had to go down with a ladder
> and set fire to all the coals down there.  One day the phone
> rang and Mr. Dalton asked me to come up and see [5778] as
> he called him, for he said he had fallen over in [[Well Shaft (Pyramid)|the pit]].
> He had tumbled over a fainting spell, and there he was
> unconscious.  He was breathing, but he had had a "spell."
> Two or three colored boys lifted him up and carried out and
> laid him under a tree.  Then we piled some straw on a road
> wagon and took him to his home.  That was the first case of
> malnutrition that I ever saw.  Cases like his were very rare,
> and when I first saw him I had no idea what the diagnosis
> was.  Prior to this the only pellagra I had ever heard of
> was in Italy.  Italy was full of pellagra - in other words,
> malnutrition - and it was brought about by improper foods.
> This was the first case I ever saw and it had been overlooked
> until Cayce explained the exact approach.  Then it was very
> easy to diagnose that it was exactly the same as a number of
> cases at the hospital, but I didn't know about them at that
> time.  Up to then it was a puzzle; but every puzzle is simple
> after it has been explained.
> 
> At this time Cayce was living in [[Hopkinsville|Hopkinsville]], [[Kentucky|Kentucky]],
> where he had moved from Bowling Green, [[Kentucky|Kentucky]].  We set him
> up in [[Hopkinsville|Hopkinsville]] - bought him an outfit - prettiest stuff
> you ever saw!  Dandy new [[Photography (Atlantean)|camera]] and all, must have spent
> the biggest part of five thousand dollars just to outfit
> his office.
> 
> When I went in about this [5778] case, Cayce laid down on the
> cot and after he laid there awhile he explained that it was
> all a case of malnutrition.  After going into detail, he
> named the different foods needed in the process of bringing
> him back to health.  The next day after this Cayce reading
> I saw Dr. Board coming down the street.  I called to him and
> said, "Dr. Board, you're quite the talk in western [[Kentucky|Kentucky]]
> and I've got a fellow out here I want you to see.  He's right
> on the road on the way out to the hospital."  He said he'd be
> glad to go.  I had gotten my new car just a few weeks before
> - the first one I ever had - this was in 1905 or '06.  So
> we drove out to see this [5778] and we went in and looked
> at him, and Dr. Board tried to get a line on him.  After he
> got through he said, "Have you got plenty of time?"  I said,
> "Sure."  He said, "Let's go on out to the hospital.  We've
> got four cases out there exactly the same as this.  My boys
> have been wrestling with them for a year and they have never
> diagnosed them."
> 
> "Now I had had Cayce's diagnosis, but Cayce didn't name it at
> all.  Cayce had said the man was "slightly off up here."  He
> had functioned with malnutrition and then they may go out
> of their heads and they are skinny and simply a case of
> malnutrition.  This [5778] was one of the first cases
> reported in the United States.  Dr. [[Milton Harrison|Milton]] Board read a paper
> before the Kentucky Medical Society and presented his cases
> out there at the hospital.  But he didn't tell anything about
> Edgar Cayce, though!  See the point?
> 
> Well, when Dr. Board and I were having lunch at the hospital
> he said to me, "Did you say you had a diagnosis for this
> case?"  I said, "Yes, but I'm not going to tell you!
> You're an old practitioner and I'm just an embryo!"
> 
> He said, "Well, where did you get it?"
> 
> I told him I picked up a new medical book the other day,
> published by a Philadelphia concern, and the description was
> a perfect description of my case, and also of the four cases
> he had.  He turned to his wife and asked her if he had a copy
> of that book.  She said he had and she went and got it for
> him.  He opened it up, and right here - "PELLAGRA"!  He said
> to me, "But how did you get..."
> 
> I said, "My 'boy', Cayce."
> 
> He said, "The hell you say!"
> 
> I said, "That's the truth."
> 
> He said, "You mean that fellow diagnosed that case for you?"
> 
> I said, "That's the truth."
> 
> Now, this Dr. Board was a member of the Kentucky Medical
> Society when they had me on the spot.  They had heard
> of Cayce, but they were afraid of him because he was
> "different."  Like Billy Mitchell.  Too far ahead of his time
> to be acceptable.  So Dr. Board read his paper and it was
> published in the Kentucky Medical Journal, and from that
> copied into the A.M.A. Journal.  Then they began picking up
> cases of Pellagra from all over where the people had been
> eating nothing but hog and hominy.  The papers were full of
> it for months.  Now-a-days you don't hear anything about it
> anymore, but back then it was quite a problem.  Just like
> Cayce said right out in his reading, "Too much hog and
> hominy!"  Said he needed vegetables and a well-balanced diet,
> which all pellagra patients respond to.  [5778]'s case was
> just as typical as I ever saw anywhere, and he finally died
> because he was too weak to get well.
> 
> I got together with Cayce in good cases, bad cases, mediocre
> cases, and all kinds.  Surely if I had a case that I didn't
> know what it was, or if it was different from the others,
> I would go over and ask Edgar if he would "sleep" on it.
> What he said wasn't written down in those days, but I
> could remember it all.
