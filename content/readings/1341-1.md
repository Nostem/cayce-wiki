Date: 2/25/1937  Sex: M  Age: 54  ReadingID: 7013

This Psychic Reading given by Edgar Cayce at his home on Arctic Crescent, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 25th day of February, 1937, in accordance with request made by Mr. [1151], Active Member of the Ass''n for Research & Enlightenment, Inc.

Time of Reading 
3:35 to 4:15 P. M. Eastern Standard Time.     [[New York City|New York City]]. 
(Life Reading Suggestion)

1. EC:  Yes, we have [[Records (Atlantean)|the records]] here of [1341].  Many associations we find that come from out of connections There is a relationship here between large orchard grounds and [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the entity]] near [[Ohio|Cincinnati]] that should be looked into by [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the entity]].  There is an association with an old estate in [[England|England]] whose coat or crest is the unicorn, the helmet - yes, two unicorns, the helmet and staff and the plume about the staff.  These are parental (?) associations of material natures.  Yes, there was a railroad, too, with it.

2. In giving that as we find from [[Records (Atlantean)|the records]] here of [[Entity (Cayce Term)|the entity]]'s activities in its [[Incarnation|sojourns]] in the earth and those interims of the environs about the earth, we find little of an [[Astrology|astrological]] aspect becoming a portion of the entity's experience.  Not that the entity may be said to be wholly material-minded, but the application of the mental self has been and is held to be so necessary in the material things that the spiritual is too easily lost sight of in practical application by the entity.

3. As we find then from a summation of the experiences (as we shall see), and from the application of self as related to the material, the mental and spiritual applications, this might be said of the entity:

4. While of sufficient mien to enjoy a good joke, to be laughed at or derided by others is unbearable to the entity; oft arising, or causing the mental exertions of the entity to produce a determination to override - by the very mental abilities - such that would cause or bring such experiences in the affairs or dealings of the entity.

5. Hence while the activities are very good in associations of political and economic conditions, affairs dealing with national and international relationships, this very experience or condition must be well cared for by the entity, lest this become a stumblingstone to those projects, those conditions as the entity may interest self in for the common welfare of the many.

6. Then it is necessary for not merely a mental patience (for this the entity possesses) but spiritual patience also.

7. In the [[Astrology|astrological]] urges we find that Venus, Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune deal with latent urges; though these remain the more oft latent, unless there is a greater application of those influences in those directions as indicated.

8. Hence the entity is "a good fellow well met," easily making friends; yet in the very nature is reserved in a manner.  A good conversationalist, and he should make himself a better listener.

9. Do not continue to make for justification by reason alone materially, but justification by deeds done and by spiritual application of [[Ideal|ideals]] more than ideas will bring a greater ability of holding the interest and of making practical the great mental abilities that are shown in the entity's study, the entity's speech, the entity's activities in many ways.

10. This makes also for the greater interest mentally manifested in the many; and for the abilities to interest those who have been or who are upon waters, or overseas.

11. Hence international as well as national subjects become a portion of the entity's experiences.

12. And when that type or element of patience is manifested, those activities with those influences dealing with international problems will become the greater interests of the entity.

13. As to the [[Resurrection Appearances|appearances]] in the earth, that influence the entity in the present, we find:

14. Before this the entity was in the earth during those periods of what is now called the American Revolution, during those activities of the Continental armies in portions of now [[New York City|New York]], Connecticut, Hampshire and Vermont - in the name Wayne.

15. We find the entity was then of what would be called today the Intelligence Department of that portion of the Continental Armies; reporting to, making for activities especially with Mercer, Washington, Greene, and those of that part of the Army activities.

16. The entity gained through the greater portion of the experience, and remained in active force in those portions of the land after there were the settlings of the land.

17. From those experiences the interests then come in the ability to gather data or facts from those various projects, whether they be of a national or international nature, whether they be for commercial organizations or for a state, city, or the national projects that may be begun or attempted.

18. Hence not as a mere statistician but rather as one that would supply data for statisticians, for those in the greater authority.

19. And because of that very weakness the entity may be the greater service in the background than in the van of any great movement; though first overcoming or satisfying or basing the [[Purpose|purposes]] of the activity upon spiritual imports and none of self, the entity may be a leader.

20. Before that we find the entity was in the land now called the [[Rome|Roman]], during those periods when there were the greater expansions under Claudius Caesar for an empire that was to rule not only most of the activities of the world in military and economic life but in the moral life.

21. The entity then was among those of the emissaries who represented the reporting to those in authority under that Caesar; especially in the [[Greece|Grecian]] land, the Mesopotamian land, and a portion of the [[Palestine|Palestine]] land.

22. There the entity gained, there the entity lost.  For with the taking on of the entity's experiences with the teachings of [[Paul (Apostle)|Paul]] in portions of the land, in the acceptance of same, the entity gained; yet with being laughed at for his tender heartedness, his chicken-heartedness even as called then, it became the downfall or the losing portion of that experience - as Tultimustouen, or Timous as was generally termed in the experience.

23. From that experience and those activities, we find in the present those inclinations for the questioning of those who rely too much (as judged by the entity) upon their religious affiliations or associations; and yet not tolerant enough is the fault in these directions.  Though there is the holding to tenets, the entity makes them as mental abilities rather than giving their proper evaluation in the affairs and in the lives and in the promptings of the fellow men.  This became - and is yet - a condition, an experience, to be met.

24. Do it NOW!

25. For without the Lord guide, indeed may the greater mind fail - for it must!

26. Before that we find the entity was in the land now known as the Indian, or the Indian and [[Egypt|the Egyptian]]; when there was the coordinating of those of the lands of [[Lemuria|Mu]] and of Saneid and [[Ra Ta|Ra-Ta]] and On, all in their attempts for that purposefulness which is a harkening to something within the entity.

27. For the entity was an emissary and visited most of those and saw how that in the moral life and in the spiritual life there might become a unison, and peace and harmony made to be the coordinating forces in all the activities of the peoples of the earth.

28. So the entity becoming one with that [[Purpose|purpose]] in self, and in the name Egoan, made for the first unison - and then of self-indulgence.

29. These that the entity made that become disrupting, meet them - first in self.

30. As to the abilities of the entity then, and that to which it may attain:

31. There are those experiences, as indicated, wherein the entity may accomplish much; or that whereunto the entity may accomplish is only limited to that wherein the entity limits the [[Purpose|purpose]].  For if the purpose is builded in [[Creative Forces|Creative Forces]] and spirituality rather than any economic, political or by partisan forces, the activities then become as helpmeets with the Creator of harmony in and among men.  But if there is the self injected, then these must make for hard experiences.  For the greater the ability, the greater is that required or demanded not only of and by self, but self's relationships to [[Creative Forces|Creative Forces]] as may manifest in a material world.

32. In those things as pertain then to the national and international relationships as to law and order for a DIVINE purpose may the greater forces come, materially - that in the financial and in [[Law of Moses|the law]] may the entity find justification.

33. (Q)  Was the entity [1341] connected with or related to the entity [1151] in any previous life?
(A)  In the [[Rome|Roman]] experience.  They were then as co-laborers for a purpose.

34. (Q)  Was the entity [1341] connected with an entity named BUCKLE during the nineteenth century?
(A)  Do not find so, save as a helpmeet for a Buckle - or Buckalew.

35. (Q)  Is it intended that the entity [1151] maintain close personal relations with the entity [1341]?
(A)  This may be very well for [1341]!

36. (Q)  Was the entity [1341] related previously to the entity [...], his wife?
(A)  That's a part of the relationships with the [1341]'s of [[Ohio|Cincinnati]], [[New York City|New York]] - yes, and of those places near Edinburgh.  Close relationships but rather blood, as would be called, than marital - yes, close relationships.

37. (Q)  Will the entity [1341] crystallize his [[Dreams|dream]] of an organization devoted to international peace, at an early date?
(A)  Conquer self, and he may!

38. (Q)  Any other information?
(A)  Much might be said, much is bound about by self.

39. We are through for the present.

> 
> This was the one of whom it was said:  "Out of him cometh
> forth the corner stone, out of him the tent nail, out of him
> the battle bow, out of him every ruler of others together.
> And they shall be like mighty men, treading down their
> enemies in the mire of the streets in the battle, and they
> shall fight because the Lord is with them, and the riders on
> horses shall be made ashamed."  The [[Old Covenant|Old Testament]] seers were
> neither the first, nor the last to lay down the principle of
> everlasting life.  Socrates said that death is only the
> separation of the soul from the body; that the intelligence
> is soul, like the Divine Mind, and both are immortal; that
> we recollect afterward things which we acquired before our
> birth; that "if the soul exists before birth and when it
> comes into life and is born from anything else than death and
> a state of death, must it not also exist after dying, since
> it must be [[Reincarnation in the Bible|born again]]."  "These souls," he said, "flit about
> until, through the desire of the corporeal which clings to
> them, they are again imprisoned in a body."  And again:  "God
> and the principle of life and everything that is immortal can
> never perish.  The soul being immortal is also imperishable."
> Aristotle says:  "Now though only one of the powers of the
> soul, intellect alone of these powers has no bodily organ; it
> alone in immortal; it alone is divine."  In the Upanishads
> in the seventh century B.C. Death answers Nachiketas, "The
> knowing self is not born; it dies not; it sprang from
> nothing; nothing sprang from it.  The ancient is unborn,
> eternal, everlasting.  If the slayer thinks that he slays, or
> if the slain think he is slain, they do not understand, for
> this one does not slay nor is that one slain."  Philo of
> [[Alexandria|Alexandria]] before Christian thought had perfected itself and
> Giardano Bruno in later times attested to the same belief.
> Hume says:  "Metempsychosis is the only system of immortality
> that philosophy can hearken to."  Bulwer Llyton declares:
> "Eternity may be but an endless series of those migrations
> which men call deaths, abandonments of home after home, ever
> to fairer scenes and loftier heights.  Age after age the
> spirit may shift his tent, fated not to death in the dull
> elysium of the heathen but carrying with it evermore its two
> attributes, activity and energy."  And Schopenhauer makes
> this statement:  "We find the doctrine of metempsychosis
> springing from the earliest and noblest ages of the human
> race, and always spread abroad on the earth as the belief of
> the great majority of mankind."
> 
> The character and genius of those mentioned as of the line of
> David reborn from life to life are the same.  The greatest of
> military strategists, statesmen by being builders of unity
> and order, law-givers, writers and even orators, they, or
> more properly speaking the last mentioned as Bonaparte.
> Potentially, the personal characteristics in each life are
> similar.  If we would demur that Attila was unlike Alexander,
> Caesar or Bonaparte, let us consult Creasy, who in his
> "Fifteen Decisive Battles of the World" says:  "When we turn
> from the legendary to the historic Attila we see clearly
> that he was not one of the vulgar herd of barbaric
> conquerors.  Consummate military skill may be traced in his
> campaigns; and he relied far less on the brute force of
> armies for the aggrandizement of his empire than on the
> unbounded influence over the affections of friends and the
> fears of foes, which his genius enabled him to acquire.
> Austerely sober in his private life, - severely just on the
> judgment seat, - conspicuous among a nation of warriors for
> hardihood, strength and skill in every martial exercise, -
> grave and deliberate in counsel, but rapid and remorseless in
> execution, - he gave safety and security to all who were
> under his dominion, while he waged a war of extermination
> against all who opposed or sought to escape from it."  Arrian
> says of Alexander:  "As to those pleasures which regarded the
> body, he showed himself indifferent; as to the desires of the
> mind he was insatiable."  Helland Rose says of Bonaparte:
> "In spite of his prodigious failure, he was superlatively
> great in all that pertains; to government, the quickening of
> human energies and the art of war...His greatness lies, not
> merely in the abiding importance of his undertakings, but
> still more in the titanic force that he threw into the
> inception and accomplishment of all of them - a force which
> invests the storm-blasted monoliths strewn along the latter
> portion of his career with a majesty unapproachable by a
> tamer race of toilers."  Sheshonk founded a new dynasty in
> [[Egypt|Egypt]] by his own capacity, conquered Israel, Judah and Nubia,
> and developed his kingdom to great prosperity.  During the
> reign of thirty-five years of Shalmonessar II over Assyria,
> [[Babylon|Babylon]] was reduced to vassalage by his military prowess.
> Great in soldierly and administration qualities the second
> Sargon by his own capacities became king of [[Babylon|Babylon]].
> Psammeticus was a firm and wise ruler under whom [[Egypt|Egypt]]
> recovered its prosperity and extended its boundaries.  [[Greece|Greece]]
> was saved at Marathon by the genius of Miltiades.  Athens
> lost the war with Syracuse because it failed to make use of
> the talents of Alcibiades, and Hellas finally triumphed
> through Alexander.  Philadelphus was the greatest of the
> Ptolemies and patronized scientific and literary research.
> Hannibal was not only a great warrior, but a statesman and a
> man of resource.  The innate military prowess of Mithradates
> I enabled him to succeed his brother on [[Throne of God|the throne]] and extend
> the frontiers of Parthia over [[Babylon|Babylon]], Bactria, Iran and
> Media.  And it was the same intuition and memory that led
> Tiberius, Trajan, Septimus Sereus, Aurelian, Maximin and
> Julian to rise by military steps and solely by individual
> merit to the purple.
> 
> Finley says "the fame of Heracleus would have rivalled that
> of Alexander, Caesar or Hannibal had he expired at [[Jerusalem|Jerusalem]]
> after the successful termination of the [[Persia|Persian]] war.  He
> had established peace throughout the Empire, restored the
> strength of the [[Rome|Roman]] government, revived the power of
> Christianity in the East, and replanted the holy cross on
> Mount [[Crucifixion|Calvary]].  His glory admitted of no addition."  Of Leo
> the Isaurian Foord writes:  "He opened his reign with the
> most splendid victory in history, saving his realm and
> religion from destruction, once more staving off from Europe
> an attack which could not otherwise have been resisted; out
> of the wild chaos about him he built up a fresh and in many
> respects an entirely new structure of Europe, throwing into
> the tremendous task a force and enduring energy, a stern and
> pure religious enthusiasm.  Where he inherited ruin and
> misery, he left strength, order, peace and reviving
> prosperity."
> 
> Justinian, who reformed the legal and administrative system
> of the empire and instigated its wars and ecclesiastical
> reforms, was a man of remarkable bodily and mental energy.
> The reign of Harun al Raschid was the most brilliant in the
> history of the Caliphate.  Scholar and poet, he surrounded
> himself with learned men.  Zinghis [[David Kahn|Kahn]] and Timur subdued
> Asia, but possessed the traits and likeness of Caesar and
> Bonaparte.  Spain was united by the statesmanship and
> military talents of Alphonse VII.  The executive and
> diplomatic genius of Gregory VII laid the foundations of
> papal power for centuries.  Though the life of Boniface
> VIII fell in a more unfortunate time, he evinced the same
> qualities of leadership.  Alfred and Canute were alike as
> simple warriors and statesmen, loved by their followers.
> Casimir IV of Poland was one of the greatest statesmen of
> his age.  His habits were simple, his political sagacity
> profound.  Creasy says of Suleiman the Magnificent:  "We must
> remember his princely courage, his military genius, his high
> and enterprising spirit, his strict observance of the laws of
> his religion without any taint of bigoted persecution, the
> order and economy which he combined with so much grandeur and
> munificence, his liberal encouragement of art and literature,
> his seal for the diffusion of education, the conquests by
> which he extended his empire, and the wise and comprehensive
> legislation with which he provided for the good government of
> all his subjects."  Hugh the Great was the real founder of
> the dynasty of the Capets.  He might have made himself king
> had he chosen, but, instead, nominally supported the last of
> the Carelingians while himself exercising all real power.
> Cardinal de Retz says that Turenne from his youth up
> possessed all good qualities.  He was a warrior and of
> heroic mould.  Montecuculli, his opponent and enemy of the
> battlefield, said:  "Today a man has fallen who did honor to
> man," and uncovered respectfully.
> 
> And yet we must not regard any of these men who contributed
> so much to civilization as great.  We think of David as the
> author of the [[Psalms|Psalms]] more than as the warrior and statesman
> who unified Israel.  His simplicity in the presence of God
> has endeared him to Christendom.  Alexander appeals to us
> also as the pupil of Aristotle and because he took with him
> to [[India|India]] a body of savants who were to advance civilization
> alone with the sword.  Caesar, said by Merivale to be the
> greatest name in history, is known to every school boy mainly
> as the author of the "commentaries."  And the youthful
> Bonaparte, emulating Alexander, took with him to Egypt a
> number of scholars who were [[Destiny|destined]] to discover the Rosetta
> stone, unlock the learning of the ancient East and found the
> study of comparative religions.
> 
> If Caesar were to walk in upon us today, diverted of all
> pomp, power and reputation, attired in the clothes of our
> time, it is quite unlikely that we should regard him as
> great, though we might think he had a certain air of
> distinction.  But, even so, the distinction would arise
> solely from his personality.  We might agree with Mommsen
> that he was a gentleman and a man of genius and that he had
> a heart.  Anything else would require the time and the
> opportunity to call forth those latent forces within his
> nature, which, added to inspiration from without, could make
> him again an instrument for the development of humanity.  We
> should find the contour of his features the same.  And we
> should find this to be so because the spirit gives form to
> the body.  We know as the result of careful analysis that
> traits of character are depicted in the face.  Indeed, we
> have a new type of professional activity in personnel experts
> who tell by the physiognomy who and who not to employ.  We
> know that characteristics are shown in the shape and lines of
> the hand.  We know that temperament is shown somewhat by the
> structure of the body itself.  It should not be difficult,
> then, to go a step further, and grasp the meaning of [[Isaiah|Isaiah]]
> when he said:  "Behold, I shall remove [[Veil of the Temple|the veil]] from off all
> faces and destroy death to eternity, saith the Lord."
> 
> Caesar, or shall we say Bonaparte?  was only one of many
> geniuses who have profoundly influenced civilization and made
> the story of history.  If we have an intuition that one of
> these was the other how shall we prove it?  We know that
> there is a law of continuity of form, that is that form in
> nature is continued in its own form, as the tree, the flower,
> the dog and the man.  We know that there is a law of
> continuity of being, that the spirit which is imperishable,
> because now in a body as a process of nature and giving its
> differentiating features the form they possess, must have
> been in a body before and must be in a body again.  We must
> depend upon our intuition that one is the other, an intuition
> which has come to us without reasoning or comparison, and
> then if the features are the same and the character and
> powers are similar we may conclude that [[Law of Moses|the law]] of
> probability is in our favor.  But if we can trace the course,
> through century after century, wherever they had opportunity,
> of all the great Builders, whether in large affairs, art or
> letters, and each one of the instances corresponds perfectly
> in contour of physiognomy, character and powers, then we may
> conclude that our theory is science and that the basis of
> history is spirit, personality, intelligence, leadership.
> 
> And we may conclude likewise that this is the basis of time,
> space, the universe.  We do not see God because God entirely
> obliterates self in incessant effort for good everywhere.  If
> we look into the sprouting crops, the restless ocean waves,
> the stars twinkling in the night, the puppies and children
> playing, the application of electricity and steam we see
> constant activity.  We must understand, then, that one of the
> attributes of the universe is activity.  The eternal hills of
> earth, the gaseous mass of the nebulae seem changeless, but
> investigation of other worlds and nebulae in differing stages
> of growth reveal that all are constantly active.  Empires wax
> and wane, formal religions rise and fall, "our little systems
> have their day and cease to be, for they are but trembling
> lights of Thee."  But all are moving slowly but surely from
> lower to higher forms, from the apparent triumph of decadent
> form and moral wrong to the ultimate survival of the fit.
> Therefore we know that another attribute of the universe is
> growth, or evolution.  Constant activity and growth toward
> what?  If we look about us and study the history of mankind
> we find that the highest aspirations we have toward
> perfection are expressed in justice, love, kindness, mercy,
> unity and the general welfare.  If we look outside of
> ourselves in the realm of objective nature we see a constant
> development for the good of the whole.  The spirit of the
> whole in man necessitates justice, kindness, love, mercy,
> unity and the general welfare.  The spirit of nature outside
> ourselves necessitates the spirit of the good of the whole.
> Hence we may reason that the attributes of the universe
> are justice, kindness, love, mercy, unity and the general
> welfare.  All of these attributes are spiritual.  Back of
> and through the universe is mind, the mind of God.  His
> attributes are perfect justice, love, kindness and mercy.
> 
> Nature is ever renewing himself in countless forms.  Winter
> and spring, sleeping and waking, decay and renewal, death
> and birth are but evidences of the change which precedes new
> life.  It would be as contrary to the facts to say that the
> outward form of the universe is God as to say that the body
> of man is man himself.  For just as back of the universe is
> mind so back of the body is mind.  But "back of" is self
> evidently not the proper term, for the spirit of man only
> expresses itself in and through the body to which it has
> given its form.  And so God expresses himself through nature,
> his nature.  And as all in the universe changes and renews
> form while the spirit remains forever the same, so we grow
> old and die but renew at birth with the same spirit.  Only
> God, however, is perfect.  Hence our life is forever and ever
> toward that perfection which we can never attain.  We can
> be more like him only as we are more just, kind, loving,
> merciful and therefore one with him.  And in that way only
> can we find happiness.  He is perfectly happy because he is
> perfectly just, kind, loving and merciful.  He is so because
> he effaces himself for all, His glory lies only in his work
> for the good of all.  We wander away from Him in our
> selfishness, willfulness for self, our perversion of our
> individuality and life to merely selfish ends.
> 
> We can behold God only through truth.  If we would behold the
> laws of God we must behold the laws of nature.  The scientist
> perceives in truth the means of usefulness to man.  So does
> the investor.  So does the teacher.  So does the business
> man.  The most patent of all the truths of nature is that all
> must serve the whole for the good of the whole.  The message
> of the preacher is of value only to the extent that it is in
> harmony with [[Law of Moses|the law]] of God which is the law of justice,
> kindness, love, mercy and therefore of unity.
> 
> The great enemy of truth, of the nature of God and man, is
> ignorance.  The great enemy of man is his own selfishness.
> Ignorance and selfishness go hand in hand for if we were not
> ignorant of the effects of selfishness it is unthinkable that
> we should be so selfish.  The great implements of ignorance
> are the tradition and prejudice which solemnly informed
> [[Ohio|Columbus]] that what his honest thought told him he could do
> could not be done, threatened Luther with the fate of Huss
> and Bruno at the stake, declared the tomato to be a poisoned
> apple, and still exhausts itself in the contention that
> immortality, the greatest of God's gifts to man, is to be
> arrived at by belief.  If the truth of man's universal
> immortality will emancipate his mind from ignorance and
> prejudice, it should be told in the name of God.  And with
> the proof that man's salvation from selfishness lies only in
> his own efforts toward justice, kindness, love and mercy, in
> the spirit of humanity and therefore of the whole, in the
> service of man and therefore of God, we shall come nearer
> to the fulfillment of the prophesy of old of one God, one
> humanity, one law and one state in a federation of the world.
> Genuine democracy is nowhere indicated more clearly than in
> the loss of the wealth, the power, the tyranny, the title of
> the man who may use them selfishly and yet at death can take
> with him to face the justice of God only that character of
> himself with which he must live in the next life on earth.
> From this neither dogma nor ritual can save him, for that
> which he sows will he also reap.
> 
> Nothing which a spirit has developed is lost.  A Michael
> Angelo becomes a Rodin, Moliere lives again as Voltaire,
> Plutarch as Montaigne, Francis of Assisi as Lincoln, Danton
> as [[Franklin Roosevelt|Roosevelt]], Jefferson as Wilson, Washington as Garabaldi.
> As contemplation is only the prelude to activity, it is
> unthinkable that the individual spirit should ever seek an
> endless nirvana of contemplation without rebirth.  And
> inasmuch as like causes have like effects and the universe
> has infinite worlds in infinite space, man's past and future
> are not confined to this earth.
> 
> Five thousand instances of intuition of identity of genius in
> history, supported by unmistakable likeness of physiognomy,
> personality and potentiality, should prove to any reasonable
> mind the presumption, gradually growing into a certainty of
> conviction, that each one of the billion and eight hundreds
> of millions of people now on the earth has lived before, will
> live again and can find happiness in this life and that to
> come only in the development of the best within him for the
> benefit of all.  Would not this work, for which courage and
> fortitude are required, bring us nearer to [[Ideal|the ideal]] of
> Charles Kingsley:  "A nation - and were it even possible, a
> whole world - of free men, lifting their foreheads to God and
> nature; calling no man master, for none is their master, even
> God; knowing and obeying their duties toward the Maker of the
> Universe, and therefore to each other, and that not from
> fear, nor calculation of profit or loss, but because they had
> loved and liked it, and had seen the beauty of righteousness
> and trust and peace, because the law of God was in their
> hearts; and need at last, it may be, neither king nor priest,
> for each man and each woman were kings and priests of God.
> Such a nation - such a society - what nobler conception of
> mortal existence can we form?  Would not that indeed be the
> kingdom of God come on earth?"
>

> terminate activities with Mr. [1341].
>
