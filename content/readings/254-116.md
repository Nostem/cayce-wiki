# TEXT OF READING 254-116

**Reading [[254-116]]** · Series: [[250-261 — Mental and Spiritual Development (Individual)|250-261]] · Year: 1944 · Sex: M

## Text

Date: 8/28/1944  Sex: M  ReadingID: 1254

This Psychic Reading given by Edgar Cayce at the office of [[A.R.E. (Association for Research and Enlightenment)|the Association]], Arctic Crescent, [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., this 28th day of August, 1944, in accordance with request made by those present.

[Edgar Cayce; [[Gertrude Cayce|Gertrude Cayce]], Conductor; [[Gladys Davis|Gladys Davis]] & Jean Fitch, Stenos.]

Time of Reading 
Set bet. 3:30 and 4:30 P. M. Eastern War Time.

1. GC:  You will have before you the work of the Ass'n for Research and Enlightenment, Inc., [[Virginia Beach|Virginia Beach]], Va., especially in reference to the Building Fund recently started for the [[Purpose|purpose]] of erecting another building, or annex, where the work as a whole might be carried on more efficiently and on a much more extensive scale.  You will consider the letter drafted by Mrs. Ethel Clyde, a copy of which I hold in my hand, and advise if it would be well to send this to all our members.  If so, what amount should be specified as the goal? Should anything else be added?  You will answer the questions which may be asked:

2. EC:  In considering the work of [[A.R.E. (Association for Research and Enlightenment)|the Association]] for Research and Enlightenment there are many phases of same which need consideration.  As we have oft indicated the publications should be first.  This department is receiving some consideration.  It is not yet altogether set as to the best plans.

3. As for the research, to many this is the most important part. To be sure, many papers, many pamphlets should come out of the research.  Under present circumstances there is not sufficient room for this to be carried on for the work of the readings, the filing, the compiling of that necessary which may be drawn from the data - there is not sufficient room. Then the building fund is to be considered.  To be sure this is hardly plausible in its entirety until cessation of war activities - and you will have cessation before the snow flies even in [[Russia|Russia]]!

4. The manner of the letter is very good provided it is handled entirely from the office or home of Mrs. Clyde.  Then, let her send envelopes to be addressed - and the amount set at $15,000, with those requests from $1 up, the present amount being already near $500.  This fund is to be kept separate and sent, as indicated, to the Secretary (or Treasurer) of the Building Fund, see?

5. (Q)  How often should the Building Fund Treasurer [Beatrice Seay] report in the Bulletin the amount reached?
(A)  At least once every two months or whenever there is the request for same.  The ones to whom letters or the envelopes are addressed should be culled by [[Gladys Davis|Miss Davis]].

6. (Q)  How should it be determined which members should receive the letter?
(A)  All who have asked for membership and especially those who have indicated benefits derived.  Those who are interested the greater in other phases should be among the ones indicated, that they may have the opportunity.

7. We are through.

Copy to Mrs. Clyde
"    " Ass'n file "   for indexing

> EDGAR CAYCE, 1877-1945 [Editorial by [[Thomas Sugrue|Thomas Sugrue]]]  [See

> Edgar Cayce passed from this life on January third at 7:15
> o'clock in the evening, at his home on Arctic Crescent,
> Virginia Beach, Virginia.  Funeral services were conducted at
> the residence on Friday morning, January 5th, by the Rev.
> Jos. B. Clower, former pastor of the local Presbyterian
> Church, and in his native town of [[Hopkinsville|Hopkinsville]], [[Kentucky|Kentucky]], on
> Monday morning, January 8th, by the Rev. Monroe G. Schuster,
> pastor of the First Christian Church.
> 
> He had been ill since August 1944, when the strain of
> overwork pressed him down to a sick bed.  For more than a
> year he had worked under unbelievable handicaps.  His son,
> the manager of the Association for Research and
> Enlightenment, [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn Cayce]], entered the Armed Services.
> At the same time an unprecedented number of requests for
> readings came to Virginia Beach.  Often there were more than
> 500 letters in the daily mail.  The library, which had
> seemed so large when first built, was jammed with
> stenographers working at correspondence.  Mr. Cayce himself
> examined every letter, and worked late into the night
> dictating answers.
> 
> During the morning and afternoon periods he gave not two but
> from eight to twelve readings.  Still he could fulfill but a
> small portion of the applications.  The others, with their
> tales of misfortune and suffering, weighed heavily on him.
> For the first time in his long life of service he could not
> help everyone of his fellows who asked for aid.  He worked
> harder and harder, but the appointments ran on and on, until
> they were more than a year ahead of him.
> 
> His own diagnosis, given in a reading last September was that
> he had reached a point of complete nervous exhaustion.  From
> this he did not recover.
> 
> As this is written the shock of his passing is so deep that
> even the most glib tongues are stilled.  Once a person came
> to know Edgar Cayce, he thereafter could not imagine a world
> without him, without his readings, without his personality,
> his friendliness, his simple and complete Christianity.  The
> roll of those he served is long.  How many he reached through
> his readings, through his sixty years of Bible teaching, is
> incalculable.  His only ambition was that after his death
> even more people be reached and given whatever in the
> readings was good and helpful.
> 
> That is the first reaction to his death - that faith must be
> kept with him, and his work continued.  It is symbolic that
> his own passing was brought about largely by the change in
> his work; that is, he could no longer give all the readings
> which were requested of him, and the necessity for giving to
> people the information of general use in the readings was
> growing each day.
> 
> Now that he is gone this will become the whole of the work,
> and as it proceeds the real stature of Edgar Cayce will be
> revealed.  Truly he fulfilled the Christian [[Ideal|ideal]]; he laid
> down his life for his friends.  He was a great man.  We shall
> not see his like again.
>

> GERTRUDE EVANS CAYCE
> 1880 - 1945
> On Easter Sunday morning at 7:15 o'clock, in her home on
> Arctic Crescent, Virginia Beach, Virginia, Gertrude Evans
> Cayce passed away.  She was buried at Hopkinsville, [[Kentucky|Kentucky]],
> in the family plot which three months before had been opened
> to receive her husband.
> 
> Thus ended the love story of Gertrude Evans and Edgar Cayce.
> It began on a hot summer day in the early eighteen-nineties,
> in Hopkinsville, where the Cayce family had come to live from
> their farm in south Christian County.  Edgar was working in
> the local book store.  One day a family friend, Miss Ethel
> Duke, drove up in a horse and buggy and called him outside.
> In the carriage with her was a pretty young girl.  "I want
> you to meet a cousin of mine," Ethel said to Edgar.  "This is
> Miss Gertrude Evans.  Gertrude this is Edgar Cayce."
> 
> That was the beginning.  He went to a lawn party at
> Gertrude's home the next week.  Soon he was visiting at the
> Hill regularly.  They became engaged, and on Wednesday,
> June 17th, 1903, they were married.  Through the years they
> worked together, and it was Mrs. Cayce who sat beside her
> husband, acting as conductor, during most of the 15,000
> readings which he gave during his long career as one of the
> world's great psychics.
> 
> Everyone who knew Mr. Cayce was amazed by his strange power,
> but those who were privileged to know the man personally,
> and to observe the family life which surrounded him, came
> eventually to be as equally impressed by the phenomenon of a
> home which seemed to achieve [[Ideal|the ideal]] which every person
> cherishes for this sacred institution.  There is no doubt
> that the Cayce family life was unique and inspiring.  Not
> only was there complete harmony between the four members of
> the family - Gertrude, Edgar and their two sons, [[Hugh Lynn Cayce|Hugh Lynn]]
> and [[Edgar Evans Cayce|Edgar Evans]] - but they had a genius for absorbing friends
> and visitors into the group in such a way that a stranger
> felt at home almost instantly.  Many families are closely
> knit, but usually in such cases the members present a united
> front against outsiders.  The opposite was true of the
> Cayces.  A friend of any one of them was friend of all four.
> Their home was always big enough for one more friend, one
> more visitor.
> 
> Probably this is explained by the fact that individually and
> collectively they tried to live Christianity.  They never
> gave lip service to an ideal; they carried it out in deed.
> But the guiding genius of this organized friendliness was
> Mrs. Cayce.  She loved her home above all things, but she
> conceived it to be a place of rest and comfort and refuge for
> not only her family, but for anyone who needed these precious
> blessings.
>

> THE EDGAR CAYCE FOUNDATION
> 
> A report from Hugh Lynn Cayce
> 
> To:  Members of the Association
> 
> In January 1946, shortly after my return from overseas, I
> began talking with Trustees and other members of the
> Association regarding the creation of a department, to be
> known as the Edgar Cayce Foundation, which would insure the
> systematic preserving, indexing and extracting of the
> readings.  This, everyone agreed, was the very heart of our
> future activities - the key to the services we are capable
> of rendering.
> 
> In the discussions which ensued, it was agreed that
> ultimately a separate benevolent corporation be formed for
> this work; for, it was felt, many people not interested in
> taking an active part in the Association work would want to
> help in preserving and preparing for use the readings which
> helped so many people.  It was considered wise also to seek
> endowment funds for this work on a long-range program, as
> the complete indexing, microfilming, copying, extracting,
> etc., would require time, skilled technical help and
> equipment.
> 
> We have proceeded to develop these plans and I believe you
> will be gratified with the results to date:
> 
> 1. Theron Helms, Douglas Johnston and Rudolph Johnson have
> worked with me in preparing a set of corporation papers
> designed to create the cooperative, benevolent organization
> which can integrate its activities with the Association and
> the Publishing Company.
> 
> I like to think of these three - the Association, the
> Publishing Company and the Foundation - as the mind, body,
> and [[Soul|soul]] of our over-all program to make full use of the
> records and the inspiration of Edgar Cayce's work which
> basically was the bringing to men everywhere a greater
> awareness of their real nature and purpose here.
> 
> 2. From a few members of the Association sufficient funds
> have been provided to make it possible for one individual to
> begin spending full time on the indexing and extracting.  It
> is recognized that other technical and clerical help will
> later be needed.  But it has been thought wise to spend
> several months in developing a systematic plan for this work
> which can be shown to all who may be interested in preserving
> and using these records.
> 
> Gladys Davis, who knows more about the history and content
> of the readings than anyone living today, has been freed from
> all other routine duties so that she can begin working
> exclusively with the reading files.  This necessitated many
> changes which I will explain later.
> 
> 3. Plans have been set in motion to hold in [[New York City|New York City]],
> early in 1947, a Foundation meeting at which this work will
> be fully discussed.  Among those with whom the plans for this
> meeting have been discussed, who will take part in it and in
> the activity stemming from it, are:  Thomas Sugrue, Margaret
> Hall, Harold J. Reilly, William F. Mitchell, Douglas T.
> Johnston, [[King David|David]] E. [[David Kahn|Kahn]], Dr. Frank P. Dobbins, Dr. Morton
> Singer and Mrs. Julia Chandler.
> 
> In relieving Gladys Davis of her many duties with the
> Association and the Publishing Company, it has been necessary
> to divide the work.  In close cooperation with the Executive
> Committee the following steps have been taken:
> 
> Theron Helms has accepted the position of treasurer of the
> Association.  He will continue to handle the books of the
> Association and the Publishing Company.  Mr. Helms has been
> interested in the Association for many years.  He has already
> rendered magnificent service (and I use this term advisedly)
> in keeping [[Records (Atlantean)|the records]].  He is practically giving his
> services gratis to aid in furthering the work.  We are
> fortunate, I feel, in having his cooperation.  The
> Executive Committee and Trustees, who have worked with him
> through the past three critical years, are fully in accord
> with his thorough, clearcut, sound, business-like approach
> to our financial requirements.
> 
> This letter also allows me to introduce Miss Dorothy V.
> Morrisette, the new membership secretary of the Association,
> who is now handling the clerical work in the office and the
> details of both the Publishing Company accounts and the
> Association membership activities.  Dorothy (as a matter of
> interest to you) had her first reading in 1938.  In all she
> had five physical readings and three life readings.  From
> time to time she has given her services gratis in assisting
> us to get reports and in handling special clerical jobs for
> the Association.  Dorothy has had years of stenographic and
> general business experience.  She has undertaken her work
> with us at a salary far below that which can command in the
> business world.  Here, too, we are fortunate in securing such
> skilled assistance.  She will work directly with Theron Helms
> in handling all of the financial records.
> 
> Just what will this mean to you as a member of the
> Association:
> 
> 1. The work of indexing and extracting (which has been going
> on bit by bit for years) will go forward systematically and
> more rapidly.  This will mean more material for articles,
> research papers, lectures, etc., and thus more information
> for you from [[Records (Atlantean)|the records]] left by Edgar Cayce.
> 
> 2. Gradually better individual extracting service can be
> rendered on the basis of the member's requests.
> 
> 3. There will be more thorough, efficient handling of the
> membership records - more prompt answers to your questions
> and better membership services.
> 
> 4. The handling of the whole financial structure of our
> organization will be more efficient.  Clear, accurate records
> and reports are essential to orderly development of this
> phase of our work.
> 
> 5. An extension of our publications, health service, lectures
> and group study will be possible, as I am able to devote more
> time and effort to the much needed organization work in these
> fields.
> 
> Early in 1947, we will present the Foundation program to you
> in a complete form.
> 
> As you know from recent reports, the Association growth

> your membership you will soon be getting a reminder from the

>

> (Q)  How did Edgar Cayce die?
> (A)  From 2/23/44 through 3/13/44 Edgar Cayce was ill,
> bordering on pneumonia.  He asked his family doctor,
> Dr. Robert Woodhouse, to try the new sulfa drug on him.
> It was a mistake.  His heart became enlarged, with fluid
> on the lungs.  8/25/44 He wrote Mrs. [1770] "such a pain
> through heart and chest".  On 8/30/44 he and Mrs. Cayce left
> Va. Beach for Roanoke for a rest.  His right hand was
> affected so that he couldn't any longer use his little
> typewriter to peck out personal letters; no strength in his
> fingers.  On 9/17/44 in Roanoke he gave the last reading,
> for himself.  GD came up for it and Dr. Henry George III.
> It described general debilitation, the using up of all his
> energies without replenishing them.  A week later he had a
> stroke and became partially paralyzed.
> 
> On 11/21/44 [[Edgar Evans Cayce|Edgar Evans Cayce]] (just home on leave from long
> duty overseas) got his father back home via new Va. Beach
> ambulance (Rescue Squad) with Dr. C. W. Irvin going up to
> come back with him in the ambulance.
> 
> Edgar Cayce was a pitiful sight coming in on the stretcher,
> so weak he couldn't speak above a whisper - and weeping - so
> glad to be home but heartbroken to be in such a fix.
> 
> On 12/19/44 Dr. Henry George III came and consulted with
> Drs. Robt. Woodhouse and Waller Taylor.  They advised that
> if EC would not go to the hospital they would have to bring
> the hospital to him; 24 hr. nursing service; oxygen;
> digitalis; sufficient atropine or belladonna to dry him up;
> blood count; blood urea; blood phosphorus; cardiograph;
> chest x-ray; etc., etc., etc.  "Don't give him anything that
> is apt to strangle him.  I think the real danger is in him
> strangling to death."  Hospital bed; etc., etc.
> 
> At 12:01 A.M. on 1/1/45 GD, who had the night shift, said
> - as they heard the horns blowing - "Happy New Year - we're
> going places and do things in 1945."  He said "Same to you
> - maybe - maybe - if the Lord is willing."
> 
> On 1/2/45 in late P.M. a beautiful deathbed scene and
> conversation between Edgar and Gertrude was witnessed by GD
> as GC was getting ready to leave her duty and GD was coming
> on duty for the night.  [See under 294-8, Par. R25, and also
> Par. R7 above.]
> 
> At 5:50 P.M. on 1/3/45 Dr. Woodhouse said all had been done
> that could be done.  His sister Annie brought him up some
> oyster stew, which he had known beforehand that she was
> making for him.  He took 2-3 sips of it.  7:15 P.M. he
> stopped breathing.
